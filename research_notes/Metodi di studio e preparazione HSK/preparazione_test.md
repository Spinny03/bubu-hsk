# Exam preparation for standardized language tests (and standardized tests in general): evidence for designing the exam-prep core of a new-HSK app

Scope note for the report writer: these notes cover test preparation, mock exams, test-taking strategies, learning from errors, test anxiety, diagnostic/adaptive assessment and score prediction, study planning toward a fixed exam date, and HSK-specific studies. Evidence-strength tags: **[STRONG]** = meta-analysis or replicated finding; **[MODERATE]** = well-designed single study or a few concordant studies; **[WEAK]** = correlational, uncontrolled, self-selected, industry, preprint or secondary summary. "Industry" = research by a test or app vendor. The session's web-search quota ran out near the end, so a few planned checks could not be made; these are listed under Gaps.

## 1. Practice tests and mock exams: format familiarity, coaching effects, score gains vs proficiency gains, washback

### Takeaway
Practice and coaching reliably raise scores, but the gains are modest. Retaking a test adds about 0.25 SD the first time and less after that, with no further gain after the 3rd administration. Commercial SAT coaching adds about 30 points in total. In language tests, gains from test-specific coaching often do not carry over to proficiency measured another way. Low-stakes practice testing with feedback, though, is one of the strongest learning techniques known (g ≈ 0.5). So mock exams should mainly serve as retrieval practice and diagnosis. They should not be the main way of raising scores, and reused items inflate scores.

### Cited Findings

**Retest/practice effects (format familiarity) — general standardized tests**
- Meta-analysis of 50 studies (107 samples, N = 134,436) of retesting on cognitive-ability tests: adjusted mean gain d = .26 from the 1st to the 2nd administration (sample-weighted d = .24). The gain from the 2nd to the 3rd administration was smaller (d = .18; corrected .20, k = 16), and Test 1 to Test 3 was d = .51 (corrected .56, k = 15) **[STRONG]** — [Hausknecht et al. 2007, J. Applied Psychology (Cornell eCommons preprint)](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content)
- In the same meta-analysis, studies with test coaching between administrations had much larger gains (d = .70) than studies without coaching (d = .24), with non-overlapping 95% CIs. In the 23 coached samples, median coaching time was only 2.5 hours. Formal instruction time did not significantly moderate the gains — [Hausknecht et al. 2007](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content)
- Identical forms produced larger gains (d = .46) than alternate forms (d = .24), consistent with memory for specific items. For identical forms the advantage shrank as the retest interval grew. The authors predicted it would equal the alternate-form effect after about 685 days. Gains were similar in operational and research settings (d = .29 vs .24) — [Hausknecht et al. 2007](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content); also [DePaul record](https://depaul.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/retesting-in-selection-a-meta-analysis-of-practice-effects-for-te/)
- Longitudinal multilevel meta-analysis covering up to 4 administrations (122 studies, 174 samples, 786 outcomes, N = 153,185): retest effects were significant, with **no further score gains after the third administration**. Moderators were ability content, equivalence of test forms, retest interval and age **[STRONG]** — [Scharfen, Peters & Holling 2018, Intelligence 67:44–66](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intell/v67y2018icp44-66.html)

**Coaching on admissions tests (SAT) — Briggs; Powers & Rock**
- Briggs re-analysed Becker's (1990) SAT-coaching meta-analysis (25 reports). Once unpublished studies and studies without control groups were removed, the model gave small, homogeneous effects: about 8.8 points on SAT-Verbal and about 15.7 points on SAT-Math (8.8 + 6.9), on a scale where each section has SD = 100. The randomized studies in Becker's review averaged only about 12 and 14 points (SAT-M and SAT-V). Becker's model mispredicted 24 newer studies by ±17 points (V) and ±29 points (M) on average **[STRONG/MODERATE]** — [Briggs 2005, Evaluation Review 29(2):87–127](https://www.colorado.edu/education/sites/default/files/attached-files/Briggs_Meta-Analysis.pdf)
- Briggs's best estimate of the total effect of commercial coaching (Verbal + Math combined) is "very close to … about 30 points". He bases this mainly on the two largest nationally representative studies: Powers & Rock 1999 (SAT-V +6, SAT-M +18) and Briggs 2001/NELS:88 (SAT-V +11, SAT-M +19). He also notes that some programs and student types may benefit more (for example, Kaplan 2002 SAT-M +37/+59 in a small program) — [Briggs 2005](https://www.colorado.edu/education/sites/default/files/attached-files/Briggs_Meta-Analysis.pdf); NELS analysis: [Briggs 2001, Chance](https://nepc.colorado.edu/files/Briggs_Theeffectofadmissionstestpreparation.pdf)
- Briggs also cautions that very high coaching effects come mostly from studies without control groups and from unpublished studies. This matters when judging prep-industry claims — [Briggs 2005](https://www.colorado.edu/education/sites/default/files/attached-files/Briggs_Meta-Analysis.pdf)
- A secondary press summary of Briggs's 2009 NACAC report says the average commercial-coaching effect was positive but slight, around 30 points on the then-1600 scale **[WEAK, secondary]** — [Education Week 2011](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/value-of-college-admissions-test-prep-classes-unclear/2011/02)

**Language-test preparation: score gains vs proficiency gains**
- IELTS coaching study, quasi-experimental and **not randomized**. 45 Chinese learners took a 4-week intensive IELTS course in Shanghai (18 hours of preparation per component, covering common topics, past-paper formats/lexis, model answers and test-taking strategies). 44 non-preparing controls were recruited by snowball sampling. Coached learners rose from 5.66 to 6.26 overall (+0.6 band; median +0.5; r = .58), while controls gained +0.1 (median 0). **Neither group improved on independent proficiency measures**: the Oxford Online Placement Test, a lexical-decision vocabulary test, and sentence-comprehension speed/accuracy. The authors conclude that coaching gains "did not generalise" and weaken the extrapolation from score to ability **[MODERATE]** — [Trenkic & Hu 2021, Journal of the European Second Language Association](https://www.euroslajournal.org/article/10.22599/jesla.74/)
- CET-4 study (China, 1,003 undergraduates, two months of preparation): preparation effects were significant but small, explaining **2.4% of final-score variance** after controlling for initial proficiency. The only practices that contributed significantly were "curriculum-narrowing" ones: intensive practice and memorization of specific test items and earlier test versions **[MODERATE]** — Xie 2013, Language Assessment Quarterly, as summarized in [Trenkic & Hu 2021](https://www.euroslajournal.org/article/10.22599/jesla.74/)
- TOEFL iBT in China (about 2,000 test takers): "intense and short-term practice using similar test formats" was the strongest predictor of scores. Repeating the TOEFL alone also produced small but reliable gains within one month **[WEAK/MODERATE, correlational]** — Liu 2014 and Zhang 2008, as summarized in [Trenkic & Hu 2021](https://www.euroslajournal.org/article/10.22599/jesla.74/)
- IELTS writing gains were compared across test-preparation, university pre-sessional EAP and combined courses for students entering UK universities. With many moderators analysed by neural network, there was "no clear advantage for focused test preparation" **[MODERATE]** — [Green 2007, Assessment in Education 14(1):75–97](https://researchportal.beds.ac.uk/en/publications/washback-to-learning-outcomes-a-comparative-study-of-ielts-prepar-5/) (DOI [10.1080/09695940701272880](https://doi.org/10.1080/09695940701272880)). Green's wider dataset (476 students, 15 UK institutions) showed gains of about 0.2 band. Test-like class activities correlated with score gains, yet course types produced similar gains — as summarized in [Trenkic & Hu 2021](https://www.euroslajournal.org/article/10.22599/jesla.74/)
- TOEIC, Japan (one academic year; English majors n = 216, non-majors n = 149; groups used TOEIC-prep materials, Business English or General English). Among non-majors, the TOEIC-prep group gained 47.9 Reading points against 12.3–13.4 for the other groups. Listening gains did not differ meaningfully (5.4 vs 0.3 and −7.0). Among majors there were no significant differences between treatments. Conclusion: TOEIC prep materials are "of little benefit" within a comprehensive program but may boost Reading for low-proficiency non-majors **[MODERATE]** — [Robb & Ercanbrack 1999, TESL-EJ 3(4)](https://www.cc.kyoto-su.ac.jp/information/tesl-ej/ej12/a2.html)
- IELTS score gain after 10–12 weeks of intensive English (112 students, Australia/NZ): average gain of about half a band overall, with large individual variation. Listening improved most; Reading was "somewhat less amenable to improvement"; predictors of gain differed by skill **[MODERATE, industry-funded]** — [Elder & O'Loughlin 2003, IELTS Research Reports Vol. 4](https://ielts.org/researchers/our-research/research-reports/investigating-the-relationship-between-intensive-english-language-study-and-band-score-gain-on-ielts)

**Practice testing as a learning technique (why mocks and quizzes still matter)**
- Classroom meta-analysis (222 independent studies, 48,478 students): quizzing raised achievement with **g = 0.499**. The effect was moderated by test-format consistency, material matching between quiz and exam, corrective feedback, number of test repetitions, timing and treatment duration. The results support additional-exposure, transfer-appropriate-processing and motivational accounts **[STRONG]** — [Yang et al. 2021, Psychological Bulletin](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Testing beats restudy, and recall tests give larger benefits than recognition tests, consistent with an effortful-processing account **[STRONG]** — [Rowland 2014, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)
- Learning from testing transfers to new questions with **d = 0.40** (192 effect sizes, 122 experiments). Transfer is largest across test formats and to application and inference questions **[STRONG]** — [Pan & Rickard 2018, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)
- Under high-stakes conditions, quiz performance itself was unchanged, but final-test benefits were smaller. Only low-stakes quizzes beat rereading **[MODERATE]** — [Hinze & Rapp 2014, Applied Cognitive Psychology](https://metatoc.com/papers/29252-retrieval-sometimes-enhances-learning-performance-pressure-reduces-the-benefits-of-retrieval-practice)
- Analog from medical licensing. In self-reported data from 399 USMLE Step 1 takers, taking 6–9 practice exams was associated with higher scores. This is correlational and the sample was skewed toward high scorers **[WEAK]** — [Seal, Koek & Sharma 2020, Cureus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198101)

**Washback (positive and negative)**
- Learners spread their preparation time in proportion to component weights, giving much less effort to low-weighted sections. Reporting component scores separately did not reverse this. A favourable view of the test's validity went with more engagement in both language learning and focused test preparation, but it did not remove negative washback (1,003 test takers, SEM) **[MODERATE]** — [Xie 2013, JLTA conference paper](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/does-test-design-affect-time-management-and-approaches-to-prepara/)
- For HSK washback studies (mostly positive) see section 7 — [Kong & Zhang 2024, SAGE Open](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/sagope/v14y2024i1p21582440231224599.html)

### Inferences
- A realistic pitch for an HSK app is that format familiarity and coaching give a small, one-off bump. In the cognitive-test literature that bump is about 0.25 SD on the first retest, and coaching in that literature is linked to larger gains (d ≈ .70). Coaching studies on language tests show about +0.5 IELTS band, but those gains may not reflect proficiency (Trenkic & Hu). Lasting gains have to come from language learning.
- Limit full-length mocks to a few: a baseline, a midpoint, and 1–2 shortly before the exam. Gains plateau after about 3 administrations (Scharfen), and each mock costs 1.5–2+ hours of time that could go to spaced retrieval practice. Make most practice section-level or item-level, low-stakes and with feedback (Yang; Hinze & Rapp).
- Never reuse identical mock items when estimating readiness. Identical-form gains (d = .46 vs .24) are item memory and will inflate predicted scores.
- To avoid negative washback (the curriculum-narrowing that Xie and Trenkic & Hu describe), the app should pair test-format drills with construct-building work (vocabulary, characters, listening volume) and track proficiency on items outside the past-paper pool. It should also not let learners neglect low-weighted HSK components such as speaking and translation, which are new in HSK 3.0.

### Gaps
- I found no randomized study that directly compares **full-length timed mocks under exam conditions with untimed or section-wise practice** for any language test (TOEFL/IELTS/TOEIC/DELE/JLPT/HSK). The evidence is indirect: format-consistency moderators (Yang 2021), pressure effects (Hinze & Rapp) and correlational USMLE data.
- No DELE- or JLPT-specific preparation-effectiveness studies were found. Search time went to IELTS/TOEIC/CET/TOEFL.
- Powers & Rock 1999 was not read directly; its numbers come from Briggs's 2005 table.

## 2. Test-taking strategies and test-wiseness (listening/reading MC; previewing, pictures, elimination, pacing, answer transfer)

### Takeaway
There is little rigorous evidence that strategy training raises language test scores beyond what better language skill would explain. Three findings are actionable. Previewing question stems helps, while previewing only the answer options hurts, because it encourages lexical matching. Revising answers you flagged as low-confidence tends to raise scores, so the "first instinct is best" belief is a fallacy. Test-takers allocate effort according to section weights. The theory treats test-wiseness strategies as construct-irrelevant.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen's taxonomy, as summarized in later validation work, splits test-taking strategies into language-learner strategies, **test-management strategies** (processes chosen to produce a correct answer responsibly) and **test-wiseness strategies** (answering without using the target ability). Test-wiseness use is thought to add construct-irrelevant variance to scores **[conceptual; secondary summary]** — [Language Testing in Asia article summarizing Cohen 1994/2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40468-019-0088-5)
- Previewing items in MC listening tests was compared in three formats: (a) stem + options, (b) options only, (c) stem only. **Options-only preview gave significantly fewer correct answers** than the other two, which did not differ from each other. The authors attribute this to a lexical-matching strategy (matching heard words to option words) **[MODERATE]** — [Yanagawa & Green 2008, System 36(1)](https://researchportal.beds.ac.uk/en/publications/to-show-or-not-to-show-the-effects-of-item-stems-and-answer-optio-5/)
- A video-based listening study on item preview exists (Koyama, Sun & Ockey 2016, Language Learning & Technology 20(1):148–165), but its full text and abstract could not be retrieved, so no findings are reported here — [LLT record](https://www.lltjournal.org/item/779/)
- **Answer changing.** Kruger et al. (2005) described the "first-instinct fallacy": regret from switching a right answer to a wrong one is more memorable, so people overrate their first instinct. Merry et al. (2021) analysed 157 physiology/biology exams and found changes went wrong→right more often than right→wrong. Couchman et al. (2016) found that revising answers tagged as low-confidence *immediately after answering* improved performance. Confidence judged later, after the exam, was unreliable **[MODERATE; secondary summary by cognitive scientists]** — [The Learning Scientists 2022](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2022/2/24-1)
- **Pacing and effort allocation.** Test-takers allocate preparation time in proportion to section weights (see section 1) — [Xie 2013](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/does-test-design-affect-time-management-and-approaches-to-prepara/)
- **HSK delivery facts relevant to strategy and transfer.** On the computer-based HSK, candidates type characters on a keyboard. Computer-based results arrive in about two weeks against about one month for paper-based. The old HSK 2.0 Level 3, for example, had 40 listening and 30 reading questions — [Wikipedia: Hanyu Shuiping Kaoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi). HSK 3.0 is offered both on paper and on computer — [Shanghai Municipal Government, Dec 2025](https://english.shanghai.gov.cn/en-Editor%27sPick-StudyinShanghai/20251222/0c7bcb2e14d641089ee3f1cce6005a7b.html)

### Inferences
- In listening mocks, the app should train using the preview time to read **question stems/prompts first**, not to scan options for words to match. Feedback can flag cases where a learner picked an option only because it repeated a word heard in the audio.
- Build a "flag if unsure" button into practice and mocks, and teach learners to revisit only flagged items. The data suggest this beats a "never change answers" rule.
- On paper-based HSK sittings, answer-sheet transfer is a real logistical step. On computer-based sittings it mostly disappears. The app should simulate whichever delivery mode the learner has registered for. This is an inference; exact HSK 3.0 transfer-time rules were not verified.
- Strategy content should take up little of the curriculum. The evidence (Trenkic & Hu; Xie) suggests strategy coaching raises scores without raising proficiency, which conflicts with a goal of "real proficiency".

### Gaps
- No verified evidence was found on **using pictures** in listening items, which matters for HSK 1–3 picture-matching tasks. A planned check of Ginther 2002 (Language Testing) failed because the search quota ran out.
- No controlled studies were found on **option elimination** or **guessing** in language MC tests. HSK 3.0's scoring rules, including whether wrong answers are penalized, were not verified. This must be checked against official sources.
- No empirical studies were found on **answer-transfer time** effects or on in-test pacing training.
- Chinese-language studies on HSK 应试策略 (for example, a Thai thesis on HSK4 listening preparation at Yunnan Normal University) appeared only as search snippets and could not be verified — [Li Jinyi thesis, HCU](https://has.hcu.ac.th/jspui/bitstream/123456789/425/1/LI-JINYI.pdf)

## 3. Learning from errors: error review, re-testing missed items, feedback after practice tests, error logs

### Takeaway
Corrective feedback after errors is among the best-supported features an app can have. Feedback after correct answers adds little, and explanatory ("high-information") feedback beats right/wrong marking. Errors made with high confidence are corrected especially well (hypercorrection). Re-testing missed items later, ideally spaced, combines the testing and spacing effects. I found no direct evidence for "error logs" as such.

### Cited Findings
- In learning Luganda–English word pairs (N = 258), supplying the correct answer after an *incorrect* response "increased final retention by 494%" one week later. Feedback after *correct* responses made little difference, whatever the learner's confidence **[STRONG lab finding]** — [Pashler, Cepeda, Wixted & Rohrer 2005, JEP:LMC](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.1.3)
- Multiple-choice tests can teach false knowledge through the lures. Immediate or delayed feedback after an MC test increased correct responses and **reduced lure intrusions** on a later cued-recall test, so feedback is recommended whenever MC tests are used **[STRONG lab finding]** — [Butler & Roediger 2008, Memory & Cognition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18491500/)
- Errorful learning followed by corrective feedback is beneficial. The **hypercorrection effect** means high-confidence errors are corrected more readily than low-confidence ones, probably through surprise and greater attention **[STRONG review]** — [Metcalfe 2017, Annual Review of Psychology](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf)
- Feedback meta-analysis (435 studies, 994 effect sizes, >61,000 participants): overall **d = 0.48**. High-information feedback (task, process and sometimes self-regulation level) d = 0.99; corrective feedback (right/wrong + correct answer) d = 0.46; reinforcement/punishment d = 0.24. Effects were stronger on cognitive outcomes (d = 0.51) than on motivational ones (d = 0.33; 21% of those effects were negative) **[STRONG]** — [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- Corrective feedback and the number of test repetitions both moderate the classroom testing effect (g = 0.499) **[STRONG]** — [Yang et al. 2021](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Immediate confidence tagging lets learners revise low-confidence answers profitably (Couchman et al. 2016) **[MODERATE]** — [The Learning Scientists 2022](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2022/2/24-1)

### Inferences
- After every practice set or mock, show the correct answer **plus a short explanation** (for example, the key word or grammar point, the transcript segment for listening, or the sentence that justifies the reading answer). Spend effort on explaining errors, not on praising correct answers (Pashler; Wisniewski).
- Collect a 1-tap confidence rating per item. Use it (a) to prioritize review of high-confidence errors, which are misconceptions that hypercorrection can fix, and (b) to feed the knowledge-tracing model (section 5).
- An "error log" should be an automatic re-test queue, not a passive list. Missed items, or close variants that test the same knowledge component, come back at spaced intervals until answered correctly on a delayed attempt (see section 6). This is an inference that combines the feedback, testing and spacing evidence.
- MC-heavy apps risk teaching lures. Always give feedback, and sometimes re-test missed items in recall format (type the hanzi or pinyin, or dictation), since recall-based retrieval gives larger benefits (Rowland 2014, section 1).

### Gaps
- No controlled studies were found on **error logs/error journals** for standardized-test preparation, whether in language tests or in general.
- No studies were found that specifically test feedback timing or format for **L2 listening/reading MC items** in exam preparation. The evidence comes from cognitive psychology lab and classroom studies.

## 4. Test anxiety and foreign-language anxiety: size of the problem and interventions that work

### Takeaway
Anxiety is reliably linked with lower L2 achievement (r ≈ −.36) and lower standardized-test performance. Structured, multi-session programs such as behaviour therapy work best (anxiety g ≈ −0.76, performance g ≈ +0.37). Two well-known "quick fixes" have weakened: single-session expressive writing (a 2026 meta-analysis found null effects and the 2011 Science study failed to replicate) and arousal reappraisal (small effect, d ≈ 0.20 on written cognitive tasks, plus a 2026 multi-site null replication). Frequent low-stakes retrieval practice is the most app-compatible approach. It makes memory resistant to stress, and most students report being less nervous after it.

### Cited Findings

**Size of the anxiety–performance link**
- L2 anxiety–achievement meta-analysis: 97 reports, 105 samples, N = 19,933, 23 countries, 216 effect sizes; mean **r = −.36**. Effects varied by type of achievement measure, educational level, target language and type of anxiety **[STRONG]** — [Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky 2019, SSLA 41(2):363–387](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/second-language-anxiety-and-achievement/) (DOI [10.1017/S0272263118000311](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000311))
- A 2026 study reported a similar anxiety–L2 performance correlation (r = −0.34), with mindfulness only partially mediating it (small indirect effect, β = −0.03) — [Song, Chen & Huang 2026, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1921078)
- Test-anxiety meta-analysis (238 studies since 1988): test anxiety is significantly and negatively related to standardized tests, university entrance exams and GPA, with relationships "small to moderate". Perceived test difficulty and high-stakes consequences were linked to higher anxiety, and self-esteem was a substantial predictor **[STRONG]** — [von der Embse et al. 2018, J. Affective Disorders 227:483–493](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29156362/)
- Among 250 foreign students in Wuhan, Chinese-learning anxiety was moderate, with communication anxiety dominant. Students who had studied longer and held **higher HSK levels had lower anxiety** (correlational) **[WEAK/MODERATE]** — [Basith et al. 2019, European Journal of Educational Research 8(4)](https://www.eu-jer.com/chinese-learning-anxiety-on-foreign-students)

**Interventions**
- Meta-analysis of 44 RCTs (N = 2,209) in test-anxious university students: interventions reduced test anxiety (**g = −0.76**) and improved academic performance (**g = 0.37**) at post-treatment. Behaviour therapy had the most support. CBT, study-skills training and combined interventions "show promise" but lack long-term evidence, and there are publication-bias concerns **[STRONG]** — [Huntley et al. 2019, J. Anxiety Disorders (Liverpool repository)](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/id/eprint/3032501). Conflict note: an earlier search snippet gave different figures (g = −0.64 / 0.28), probably from another Liverpool output; the repository abstract above is used.
- Mindfulness-based interventions: a 2024 meta-analysis reports an effect of −0.716 on test anxiety **[MODERATE; not read in depth]** — [Yılmazer, Hamamci & Türk 2024, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11238660/)
- **Expressive writing.** The original classroom study (writing about worries just before the exam) reported better exam scores — [Ramirez & Beilock 2011, Science (replication record)](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1199427/). It **failed to replicate** in the Social Sciences Replication Project: the pooled test was not significant in the original direction — [Camerer et al. 2018, Nature Human Behaviour](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/evaluating-the-replicability-of-social-science-experiments-in-nat/); [FLoRA atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1199427/)
- A meta-analysis of **single-session expressive writing** for test anxiety (21 studies, N = 1,457, 30 effect sizes) found anxiety r = −.05 (p = .57, negligible) and performance r = .09 (p = .02), falling to r = .06 (p = .06) once one outlier was removed. Conclusion: the literature "fails to support single sessions of expressive writing as a treatment for test anxiety" **[STRONG, very recent]** — [O'Meara & Lovett 2026, Anxiety, Stress & Coping](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2552857)
- A longer variant in a Chinese sample: 75 high-school students with severe test anxiety wrote about positive emotions for 20 minutes a day for 30 days. Their Test Anxiety Scale scores dropped substantially compared with controls (reported effect size 2.02). Exam scores were not measured, and the authors note that brief Western-style writing may not suit Chinese students **[WEAK/MODERATE, single small RCT]** — [Shen et al. 2018, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5798770)
- **Arousal reappraisal.** Telling participants that arousal improves performance raised salivary alpha-amylase and improved GRE-math scores, both on a practice test and on real GRE scores 1–3 months later **[MODERATE, single study]** — [Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock & Schmader 2010, JESP 46(1):208–212](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-8923-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- Meta-analysis of stress-arousal reappraisal and stress-is-enhancing mindset RCTs (44 effect sizes): overall **d = 0.23**. Mixed interventions d = 0.45 (k = 5); reappraisal-only d = 0.22 (k = 33); mindset-only d = 0.18 (n.s., k = 6). Effects were larger for public-performance tasks (d = 0.34, k = 14) than for **cognitive written tasks (d = 0.20, k = 30)**; the authors say these interventions are "not silver bullets" **[STRONG]** — [Bosshard & Gomez 2024, Scientific Reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w)
- A multi-institution replication (12 science courses, 7 US institutions) found a cognitive-reappraisal intervention **did not affect** self-reported test anxiety or performance, while test anxiety did negatively predict performance **[MODERATE/STRONG, multi-site replication]** — [Thormodsæter et al. 2026, CBE—Life Sciences Education](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.25-04-0055)
- By contrast, a brief intervention combining stress reappraisal with achievement goals ("Fueled and focused") increased exam scores and lowered salivary cortisol in 308 psychology students **[MODERATE, single study]** — [Journault et al. 2025, Psychoneuroendocrinology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2025.107544)
- AI-based interventions reduced academic anxiety overall (g = −0.65), but in moderator analyses the effect on **foreign-language anxiety was not significant** **[MODERATE]** — [Wang, Diao & Yu 2026, BMC Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05018-y)

**Practice testing, timed and mock practice, and anxiety**
- In classroom retrieval-practice programs (1,408 middle- and high-school students), **72%** said retrieval practice made them less nervous for unit tests and exams, and 92% said it helped them learn. This is a self-report survey, not a measured anxiety outcome **[WEAK/MODERATE]** — [Agarwal et al. 2014, JARMC 3(3):131–139](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/classroom-based-programs-of-retrieval-practice-reduce-middle-scho/)
- Participants who learned by retrieval practice were "immune" to the memory-impairing effect of acute stress induced 24 hours later, while restudy learners showed the usual impairment **[MODERATE, single lab study; a published commentary exists]** — [Smith, Floerke & Thomas 2016, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aah5067)
- High-stakes framing of practice quizzes reduced their learning benefit (see section 1) — [Hinze & Rapp 2014](https://metatoc.com/papers/29252-retrieval-sometimes-enhances-learning-performance-pressure-reduces-the-benefits-of-retrieval-practice)
- After one virtual mock exam (198 medical students, retrospective pre-post), self-rated confidence rose (median 5→6, p < .001), but **anxiety did not change** (median 8, p = .054) **[WEAK]** — [Dalavaye et al. 2026, Adv Med Educ Pract](https://doi.org/10.2147/AMEP.S558158)
- A quasi-experimental program combining mock exams with stress-management training (N = 1,000 nursing students) lowered perceived stress (12.1 vs 31.1). The two components cannot be separated **[WEAK]** — [Basyouny et al. 2026, Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000050037)

### Inferences
- The main anti-anxiety mechanism to build into the app is **frequent, low-stakes, feedback-rich retrieval practice**, which strengthens memory against stress (Smith 2016; Agarwal 2014). Avoid high-stakes framing of daily quizzes, such as harsh penalties or public failure (Hinze & Rapp). Save "exam-condition" simulation for a few mocks.
- Mocks alone probably raise confidence more than they lower anxiety (Dalavaye: weak evidence). Pair them with debriefs that show progress.
- Do **not** market expressive writing as an evidence-based exam booster; the evidence is null. A brief reappraisal or "stress can help" message before mocks is cheap and may add a small effect (d ≈ 0.20 on written tasks). Treat it as optional and A/B-test it, given the 2026 null replication.
- Learners with severe anxiety should be pointed to structured programs (behaviour therapy/CBT, d ≈ 0.76 on anxiety). An app can screen, for example with a short test-anxiety scale, and signpost; it should not claim to treat.
- HSK 3.0 makes speaking mandatory from Level 3. Since communication anxiety is the dominant form among learners of Chinese (Basith 2019), graded low-stakes speaking practice, such as recording answers privately before any evaluation, is likely to matter.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of **interventions for foreign-language anxiety specifically**; the search quota ran out before this could be checked. The only related result is the AI-intervention meta-analysis, which found no significant effect on FL anxiety.
- There is no controlled evidence on whether **timed practice** (as opposed to untimed practice) reduces exam anxiety.
- No HSK-specific anxiety-intervention studies were found. HSK 6 anxiety among Xinjiang international students appeared only as a search snippet (unverified).
- The exact von der Embse 2018 effect sizes (r values) could not be extracted; only the qualitative abstract is reported.

## 5. Diagnostic and adaptive assessment: CAT, IRT/Rasch placement, knowledge tracing, readiness and score prediction, cognitive diagnostic models

### Takeaway
An app can diagnose quickly. In the TOEIC app Santa, a 7–11-item placement test gives a first score estimate, and CAT can roughly halve test length at equal precision. Per-skill mastery is best tracked with well-engineered logistic or IRT-family models (PFA/LKT/best-LR). These match or beat deep models unless data are very large, and BKT lags behind. Readiness predictions can be useful but are imperfect. A large TOEIC app reached a mean absolute error of about 50 points on a 990-point scale from interaction data. Practice-exam regressions for USMLE reached R² ≈ .85 in a biased sample. Learners' self-assessment correlates only about .47 with measured performance. Cognitive diagnostic models for listening and reading subskills are feasible, but the field has not agreed on which model to use.

### Cited Findings

**CAT and IRT basics (for short placement tests)**
- CAT can typically be shortened by about **50%** while keeping higher precision than a fixed form. IRT makes scores from different item sets comparable. The test can stop once the standard error falls below a threshold. Limits: items need calibration samples of about 1,000 examinees, item exposure has to be controlled, and reviewing earlier items is usually not allowed **[MODERATE; aggregator summarizing Weiss & Kingsbury and others]** — [Wikipedia: Computerized adaptive testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computerized_adaptive_testing)
- **Industry example (TOEIC prep app Santa, about 780K users in Korea).** When the app is first opened, "a preliminary placement test with 7 to 11 problems" diagnoses the learner and predicts the expected TOEIC score in real time, and the prediction keeps updating as more questions are answered **[industry]** — [Lee et al. 2020, EDM, "Prescribing Deep Attentive Score Prediction…"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05021)
- **Official HSK 3.0 mock platform.** Chinese Testing Service launched it on 4 January 2026. It covers HSK 3.0 Levels 1–9 on web, iOS and Android, is "strictly aligned" with the official exam structure, and is intended for pre-preparation diagnosis, stage-based evaluation and learning-path planning — [Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, Jan 2026](https://edu.sh.gov.cn/study_en_hsk/20260107/5292266eca744a959c1db43237fbc185.html)

**Score/readiness prediction: achievable accuracy**
- In Santa, a deep attentive model predicted users' real TOEIC scores (0–990) with **MAE 49.84 points**, against 78.91 for collaborative filtering. The test set was 466 users with 519 real score reports; training used 1,302 users and 1,815 labels, with EdNet pre-training. An A/B test (50,451 vs 17,019 students) found that the more accurate predictor led to higher diagnostic-test completion, more questions answered and a higher purchase rate **[industry, EDM peer-reviewed]** — [Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05021)
- Pre-training on "assessment" signals (correctness, timeliness) cut exam-score-prediction MAE by 21.96% compared with no pre-training, and by 13.34% compared with earlier content-based pre-training **[industry]** — [Choi et al. 2020, "Assessment Modeling"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05505)
- Medical-licensing analog. A Step 1 predictor combining several practice exams, weighting each by its R² and giving more weight to tests taken closer to the exam, reached **R² = .845 and MAE = 3.8 points (1.6%)**. Validation used only 19 new responses, and the sample was self-selected and skewed toward high scorers (mean 246 vs national 231). The best single practice exam had R² = .68 **[WEAK/MODERATE]** — [Seal, Koek & Sharma 2020, Cureus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198101)
- A test-prep vendor says official NBME practice tests usually predict within about ±10 points when taken under test-like conditions, with strong individual variation **[industry, weak]** — [Blueprint Prep blog](https://blog.blueprintprep.com/medical/nbmes-how-well-will-they-predict-my-step-1-score/)
- Learners' self-assessment correlates **r = .466** with measured language performance (meta-analysis of 67 studies, 97 samples, >68,500 participants). The correlation was higher with explicit criteria, training, more items and more reliable instruments **[STRONG]** — [Li & Zhang 2021, Language Testing 38(2):189–218](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265532220932481)
- Learners misjudge what works: 72% believed massed study was better after one session, even though spacing was better for 90% of them **[STRONG]** — [Kornell 2009, Applied Cognitive Psychology](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152)
- Identical practice forms inflate retest gains (d = .46 vs .24 for alternate forms) — [Hausknecht et al. 2007](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content)

**Knowledge tracing (per-skill mastery)**
- DKT, introduced by Piech et al., uses recurrent neural networks to model student knowledge without hand-coded domain structure — [Piech et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05908)
- An empirical comparison on nine real datasets found that **logistic regression with the right features leads on moderate-size datasets, or where each student has very many interactions**. DKT leads only on large datasets or where precise timing matters most. Markov-process methods such as **BKT lag behind**. Calibration (reliability of predicted probabilities) is highlighted as crucial for downstream use **[STRONG]** — [Gervet, Koedinger, Schneider & Mitchell 2020, JEDM 12(3)](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/451)
- BKT extended with more modelling flexibility (recency, contextual sequences, inter-skill similarity, individual ability) performs "indistinguishable from" DKT and is more interpretable — [Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.02416)
- Automated feature search in Logistic Knowledge Tracing (stepwise regression or LASSO over success counts, elapsed time and recent performance at student, KC and item levels) gave models that were simpler and more accurate than DKT, SAKT and IKT **on all datasets**, often with orders of magnitude fewer parameters **[STRONG]** — [Pavlik & Eglington 2023, JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/722)
- On EdNet (Riiid/Santa TOEIC data, the largest public student-interaction dataset), logistic regression with engineered features beat deep models — [Mandalapu, Gong & Chen 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08349)
- For vocabulary scheduling, Duolingo's half-life regression cut recall-prediction error by "45%+" compared with baselines (Leitner, Pimsleur), although Leitner had the highest AUC. In an operational user study it raised daily student engagement by 12% **[industry, ACL peer-reviewed]** — [Settles & Meeder 2016, ACL](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174/)

**Cognitive diagnostic models (CDMs) for listening and reading subskills**
- An L2 listening diagnostic test (19 MC items, 500 Chinese university freshmen) modelled six attributes: sound discrimination; less frequent vocabulary/expressions; difficult structures; facts and details; main idea; and situational/cultural inference. At test level, A-CDM, LLM and R-RUM fitted comparably. At item level, mixed CDMs fitted best, implying both compensatory and non-compensatory relations among subskills. The small sample favoured reduced models over saturated G-DINA. The authors conclude that CDM choice for L2 listening "is still inconclusive" **[MODERATE]** — [Dong, Ma, Wang & Gao 2021, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.608320/full)
- The same paper reviews earlier L2 applications: Buck & Tatsuoka 1998 (rule-space, listening); Sawaki et al. 2009 (Fusion model); Lee & Sawaki 2009 (compensatory and non-compensatory models gave "strikingly similar" diagnoses on ESL reading/listening); Yi 2017 (C-RUM optimal); Meng 2013 (G-DINA) — [Dong et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.608320/full)
- A review of diagnostic classification model studies found most L2 work is on reading, with few studies on writing or speaking — [ensani.ir mini-review record](https://ensani.ir/fa/article/535679/examining-attribute-relationship-using-diagnostic-classification-models-a-mini-review)

### Inferences
- **Architecture suggestion.** Calibrate an IRT/Rasch item bank per HSK 3.0 level and skill. Run a short adaptive placement (about 10–20 items, following Santa's 7–11 items and the roughly 50% CAT saving) that stops at a target standard error. Then track mastery per knowledge component (word, character, grammar point, item type) with a logistic or PFA-style model, which is interpretable, works with little data and calibrates well. Switch to deep models only after reaching EdNet-like scale (Gervet; Pavlik & Eglington).
- **Readiness display.** Show a predicted score band with an interval, not a single number, plus a probability of passing or reaching the target level. Calibrate it against real HSK results that users report. Expect errors of several percent of the scale range: about 5% for TOEIC from interaction data (Santa), better when full-length mocks are included (USMLE analog). Never feed identical mock items into the predictor (Hausknecht), and weight recent full-length mocks more heavily (Seal et al.).
- **Do not rely on learners' self-rated readiness** (r ≈ .47). Kornell's results show that learners' own judgments of study methods are often wrong.
- **CDM-style subskill feedback** (for example "inference vs detail" in reading/listening) is attractive. However, model selection is unsettled and attributes usually have few items each, so per-attribute classifications will be noisy. Present them as tentative "focus suggestions" and confirm with targeted mini-tests.
- A single official HSK SEM sets a floor on how accurate any app prediction can be: no app can predict more precisely than the exam measures. Official HSK reliability figures could not be found (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No published HSK reliability or SEM figures, and no published HSK score-prediction or readiness models, were found.
- No CDM studies applied to HSK were found or verified. A Chinese query (HSK 认知诊断) returned nothing relevant before the search quota ran out.
- No peer-reviewed numbers were verified for Duolingo English Test CAT length or reliability; search snippets only.
- The Santa MAE comes from users who chose to report scores, so it may suffer from self-selection bias; the paper does not discuss this.

## 6. Study planning toward a fixed exam date: spacing vs cramming, planning, goal-setting, implementation intentions

### Takeaway
Spacing beats cramming, and the best gap between reviews grows with the time left until the test. One estimate puts it at about 20% of the remaining time when the test is a few weeks away, falling to about 5% when it is a year away. Learners wrongly believe cramming works better. Implementation intentions ("if–then" plans) have a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment (d = .65). Short structured planning exercises before exams raise scores modestly. When self-regulation interventions are scaled up in online courses, though, the effects shrink by about an order of magnitude and mainly raise early engagement.

### Cited Findings
- Distributed-practice meta-analysis (839 assessments in 317 experiments): the inter-study interval that maximizes retention **increases as the retention interval increases** **[STRONG]** — [Cepeda et al. 2006, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)
- In a study of more than 1,350 people (study gaps up to 3.5 months, final tests up to 1 year later), the optimal gap was about **20% of the test delay for delays of a few weeks, falling to about 5% for a one-year delay** **[STRONG]** — [Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008, Psychological Science 19:1095–1102](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- In realistic web-based flashcard study of GRE vocabulary, spacing beat massing and also beat **cramming** (massing on the last day before the test). Spacing was better for 90% of participants, yet 72% believed massing was more effective **[STRONG]** — [Kornell 2009, Applied Cognitive Psychology 23(9)](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152)
- Data-driven spaced-repetition scheduling (half-life regression) improved recall prediction and raised daily engagement by 12% — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174/)
- Implementation-intention meta-analysis (94 independent tests): **d = .65** on goal attainment. The benefits cover getting started, shielding ongoing goal pursuit from distraction, disengaging from failing courses of action and conserving capacity **[STRONG]** — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38:69–119](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)
- **Test-prep RCT.** 66 high-school students preparing for the PSAT were randomly assigned to a 30-minute written mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII) exercise or a placebo. The MCII group **completed more than 60% more practice questions** **[MODERATE, small RCT]** — [Duckworth et al. 2011, Educational Psychology](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/duckworth_self-regulation_11.pdf)
- College students who completed a 15-minute online "strategic resource use" exercise before an exam (planning which study resources to use and why) improved their exam scores by an average of about **one-third of a letter grade**. The design details (randomization, sample sizes) were not verified from the original article — [Chen, Chavez, Ong & Gunderson 2017, Psychological Science (APS release)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/thinking-strategically-about-study-resources-boosts-students-final-grades.html); [Stanford news](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/05/studying-strategically-equals-improved-exam-scores)
- Scaled real-world version (Exam Playbook in the ECoach platform): among more than 12,000 students in 7 STEM courses, users scored about **2 points** higher than non-users after controlling for standardized test scores. Effects were positive in 13 of 14 classes (about 6 points in statistics) and absent in one physics semester. Users chose whether to use it, so the comparison is not randomized **[MODERATE/WEAK]** — [Springer Nature "Behind the paper" summary of Chen et al., npj Science of Learning](https://communities.springernature.com/amp/posts/let-s-make-a-plan-translational-research-shows-effectiveness-of-rapid-exam-preparation-tool)
- **Scale warning.** Established behavioural interventions, including self-regulation interventions, were tested on 250,000 students in 247 MOOCs over 2.5 years. Effects were about **an order of magnitude smaller** than in earlier studies. Self-regulation interventions raised engagement in the first weeks but **not completion**, and ML methods could not learn effective individualized intervention policies **[STRONG]** — [Kizilcec et al. 2020, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1921417117)
- Plan quality matters: in a micro-randomized trial with children, higher-quality plans made studying the next day more likely **[WEAK, preprint]** — [Schaaf, Brod & Breitwieser 2025, PsyArXiv](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/jsmq4_v2)

### Inferences
- **Exam-date-aware scheduler.** Have the learner enter the HSK exam date at onboarding. Space reviews so the gaps scale with the time left (for example, gaps of about 1–2 weeks when the exam is 2 months away, shortening near the date), with every item reviewed at least once in the final week. This is an inference from the Cepeda ridgeline. Use per-item half-life or logistic memory models to schedule individual words and characters.
- **Anti-cramming design.** Learners believe cramming works (Kornell), so the app should make spacing the default. It could show each item's "predicted retention on exam day" instead of a raw count of today's reviews.
- **Planning features.** Offer a one-time MCII and if–then plan at onboarding ("If it's 8 pm on a weekday, then I do 20 min of HSK listening"). Add a 5-minute "exam playbook" reflection before each mock or before the final weeks (which resources and why). Prompt plan quality, for example by requiring a specific time and place.
- **Realistic expectations.** Plan to A/B-test these features. Kizilcec shows that at scale such nudges may mainly lift early engagement, not exam outcomes.

### Gaps
- Searches on **cramming vs sleep the night before the exam** returned nothing relevant before the quota ran out.
- No studies were found on **goal-setting or planning interventions specifically for language-exam preparation** (HSK/JLPT/IELTS), as opposed to general academic exams.
- The Latimier et al. 2021 meta-analysis of spaced retrieval practice was not verified.

## 7. HSK-specific evidence: preparation, score predictors, washback (English and Chinese literature)

### Takeaway
HSK washback research, from China and from Thailand, consistently finds positive washback outweighing negative. Washback is stronger for lower-level learners, and learner motivation and how learners perceive the test drive how intense it is. I found no peer-reviewed studies that measure whether HSK test preparation raises scores, nor any validated HSK score-prediction models. HSK 3.0 (9 levels, speaking mandatory from Level 3, translation added, both paper and computer formats) changes what preparation has to cover.

### Cited Findings

**Washback**
- Survey of 1,616 learners of Chinese as a second language from 25 L1 backgrounds, plus interviews: HSK influenced both the process and the results of learning, and **the positive impact outweighed the negative**. Motivation and perception of the HSK were the key factors in how strong washback was. Washback was **strong at lower HSK levels and weak at higher levels** **[MODERATE, self-report]** — [Kong & Zhang 2024, SAGE Open 14(1)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/sagope/v14y2024i1p21582440231224599.html) (DOI [10.1177/21582440231224599](https://doi.org/10.1177/21582440231224599))
- Mixed-methods study (questionnaires, classroom observation, teacher and student interviews): HSK has "a strong positive effect in terms of value and intensity", visible before and after testing in teaching content, methods and attitudes and in learner behaviour, with "some unintended outcomes" **[MODERATE]** — [Li & Hu 2025, SAGE Open 15(3)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/sagope/v15y2025i3p21582440251363774.html)
- In Thailand, the backwash of the new HSK Level 4 on Thai native Chinese teachers' teaching, and on high-school students' learning, was found to be more positive than negative **[WEAK/MODERATE, regional journals]** — [Chinese Language & Culture Journal (TCI)](https://so02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/clcjn/article/view/242412); [Journal of Sinology, Mae Fah Luang Univ.](https://journal.mfu.ac.th/index.php/jsino/article/view/13)
- Language Testing in Asia (2025) published an HSK washback study that also reports personal, test and environmental factors, with test factors weighing most for both teachers and students. The full text was behind an access redirect, so only search-snippet content is known **[unverified details]** — [Language Testing in Asia 2025, s40468-025-00370-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40468-025-00370-z)

**Anxiety and level**
- Chinese-learning anxiety among 250 foreign students in Wuhan was moderate (communication anxiety dominant) and lower among students with longer study and higher HSK levels — [Basith et al. 2019](https://www.eu-jer.com/chinese-learning-anxiety-on-foreign-students)

**HSK 3.0 facts that shape preparation**
- HSK 3.0 was released in November 2025, with the global pilot on 31 January 2026, and expands from six to nine levels: Beginner 1–3, Intermediate 4–6, Advanced 7–9. Paper-based and computer-based formats both exist. Candidates for Levels 3–6 must also register for the corresponding speaking test. Score reports are valid for two years — [Shanghai Municipal Government, Dec 2025](https://english.shanghai.gov.cn/en-Editor%27sPick-StudyinShanghai/20251222/0c7bcb2e14d641089ee3f1cce6005a7b.html)
- According to Wikipedia (secondary), the syllabus was released on 15 November 2025 and took effect on 18 November 2025; HSK 7–9 launched in March 2022. Vocabulary requirements were *lowered* at beginner and intermediate levels, speaking became mandatory from Level 3, and handwritten characters and translation were added. Cumulative word targets are L1 300, L3 1,000, L6 5,400 and L7–9 11,000; character recognition 246 / 655 / 1,940 / 3,088; character writing 0 / 250 / 700 / 1,200 — [Wikipedia: Hanyu Shuiping Kaoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi)
- Commercial guides say full implementation starts in July 2026, that most Level 4+ tests are computer-based, that translation is tested from Level 4, and that handwriting is required only from Level 5. Official confirmation was not found **[WEAK, commercial]** — [HSKLord blog](https://hsklord.com/blog/hsk-3-0-changes-2026). **Conflict:** Wikipedia's HSK 3.0 table already lists 250 characters to *write* at Level 3 (700 at Level 6), which does not fit "no handwriting before Level 5" unless typed input on the computer-based test replaces handwriting. Check the official 考试大纲.
- HSK 2.0 pass marks were 120/200 (Levels 1–2) and 180/300 (Levels 3–4), with no official pass mark for Levels 5–6 since February 2013. German and French Chinese-teacher associations dispute the official CEFR equivalences (they argue HSK 6 is about B2/C1, not C2) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi)
- A validity-oriented review of HSK 3.0's linguistic features and task structure, published before implementation, exists but could not be accessed (access denied) — [Columbia SALT journal](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/14906)
- Official HSK 3.0 mock tests for Levels 1–9 have existed on web and mobile since January 2026 (see section 5) — [Shanghai Municipal Education Commission](https://edu.sh.gov.cn/study_en_hsk/20260107/5292266eca744a959c1db43237fbc185.html)

### Inferences
- HSK washback is strongest for beginners (Levels 1–3). They are therefore both the users most likely to adopt narrow test-drilling and the ones for whom the exam can best structure learning. The app can use HSK 3.0 word and character lists and the new speaking requirement to drive broad learning rather than item memorization.
- HSK 3.0 adds mandatory speaking from Level 3, plus translation and handwriting (the exact starting levels are disputed between sources; see the conflict note above), so an HSK 2.0-style app built around listening/reading MC drills would produce *negative* washback (neglect of productive skills). Xie's finding that learners under-invest in low-weighted components points the same way.
- Official HSK 3.0 mocks exist (their pricing was not verified), so they are both a benchmark and an external calibration anchor. The app could ask users to report official mock and real HSK scores to calibrate its readiness model.

### Gaps
- **Chinese-language literature** (CNKI: 中国考试, 语言教学与研究, 世界汉语教学) on HSK 备考, 应试策略 and 反拨效应 could not be accessed. Only English and Thai-journal abstracts and search snippets were found.
- No studies were found on **HSK score predictors** (vocabulary size, character knowledge, study hours, L1 background) with effect sizes. One general snippet linked vocabulary size with Chinese proficiency, but no HSK-specific coefficient was verified.
- No study was found that measures whether **HSK test preparation** (courses, apps, mock drilling) improves HSK scores or proficiency, so there is no HSK equivalent of Trenkic & Hu or Xie.
- No HSK 3.0 section timings, item counts, scoring rules (guessing penalty), or official reliability/SEM data were verified.
- No HSK-specific CDM or knowledge-tracing applications were verified.
