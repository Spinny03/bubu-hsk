# Acquisizione L2 e tecnologia: evidenze SLA, MALL, gamification e AI/speech per un'app di preparazione HSK (adulti italofoni, da HSK 1)

Notes are in English (the final report is in Italian). Evidence tags: **[MA]** meta-analysis; **[SR]** systematic review; **[RCT]/[quasi]** single experimental study; **[obs]** observational/correlational; **[company]** funded or written by the vendor; **[preprint]** not peer-reviewed; **[secondary]** a number taken from a citing paper and not checked against the primary text. Effect sizes are Cohen's d or Hedges' g unless stated otherwise. Unless a finding is marked otherwise, the samples are English-L2 learners, mostly East-Asian EFL university students. Applying the results to adult Italian-L1 learners of Chinese (characters, lexical tones, a non-alphabetic script) is an assumption. Research cut-off: October 2026.

## 1. Comprehensible input, extensive reading and listening, graded readers, text coverage (Krashen's claims vs the evidence)

### Takeaway
Extensive reading (ER) has replicated small-to-medium benefits. Group contrasts give d ≈ 0.46–0.57 and pre–post contrasts d ≈ 0.71–0.79. The effects are largest for reading rate and for adult and EFL learners. Incidental vocabulary uptake is small per text (about 9–18% of unknown target words), so input has to be plentiful and carefully calibrated: about 98% known-word coverage for comfortable unassisted reading (95% is the minimum), and about 90–95% for listening. Krashen's input-centred ideas survive in reworded form. His strong claim that explicit learning does not feed acquisition is contradicted by the instruction meta-analyses in Q2.

### Cited Findings
- **Jeon & Day 2016 [MA]**: 49 primary studies, 71 unique samples, 5,919 participants (1980–2014). Experimental-vs-control **d = 0.57** (k = 51; 95% CI 0.46–0.68). Pre-to-post **d = 0.79** (k = 20; CI 0.62–0.96). The authors rate both as "small to medium" against Plonsky & Oswald's (2014) SLA-specific benchmarks — [Jeon & Day, RFL 28(2)](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/354); [full text PDF (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1117026.pdf)
- Jeon & Day moderators:
  - age: adults d = 0.70, children 0.52, adolescents 0.35 (adult k = 30; no ESL study had adult samples);
  - setting: EFL 0.65 vs ESL 0.38;
  - format: **web-based texts d = 0.89 (k = 10, mostly adults) vs paper 0.47 (k = 41)**;
  - integration: ER built into the curriculum 0.91 (k = 11) vs ER as part of a reading course 0.47 (k = 38);
  - program length had no significant effect (short 0.51, medium 0.59, long 0.60; only 4 long programs);
  - outcomes: reading comprehension 0.54 (k = 46), reading rate 0.83 (k = 10), vocabulary 0.47 (k = 17).

  The authors themselves warn that the k values are unbalanced — [Jeon & Day PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1117026.pdf)
- Jeon & Day criticise Nakanishi (2015) for including studies that broke ER principles, especially self-selection of books. Some of those studies were "obligatory assigned reading of long texts" — [Jeon & Day PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1117026.pdf)
- **Nakanishi 2015 [MA]**: 34 studies (43 effect sizes, N = 3,942). **d = 0.46** for group contrasts and **d = 0.71** for pre–post contrasts — [Nakanishi, TESOL Quarterly](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.157). Jeon & Day's summary of the paper adds larger effects for older participants (d = 0.67) and longer programs (d = 0.52) — [Jeon & Day PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1117026.pdf)
- **Hu & Nation 2000**:
  - Claim: 98% known-word coverage is the "desirable" threshold for adequate unassisted comprehension of fiction. 95% is only "acceptable" for minimal comprehension: some learners comprehend adequately at that level, but most do not.
  - Basis: a regression on only **66 New Zealand university students** reading one 633-word narrative. The text was manipulated to 80/90/95/100% coverage using pseudowords.
  - Sources: [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 (replication context)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.800983/full); [AILA 2021 replication abstract](https://aila2021.dryfta.com/program/abstract-archive/abstract/public/416/unknown-vocabulary-density-reading-comprehension-and-the-98-coverage-figure-replicating-hu-nation-2000); original: [Hu & Nation (repository copy)](https://doi.org/10.26686/wgtn.12560354)
- **Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011**: 661 participants from 8 countries. The relationship between percentage of vocabulary known and comprehension was roughly **linear, with no "threshold"**. The authors conclude that 98% is "a more reasonable coverage target" for academic texts — [Modern Language Journal](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2011.01146.x)
- **van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013 (listening)**:
  - 36 native and 40 non-native listeners heard informal narratives.
  - Most participants comprehended adequately at **90% coverage**, though non-natives varied widely.
  - At **95%**, comprehension was good and much less variable.
  - A 95% criterion implies 2,000–3,000 word families for listening, versus 6,000–7,000 under a 98% criterion.
  - Source: [Applied Linguistics](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/ams074)
- **Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023 [MA, incidental vocabulary]**:
  - Scope: 24 studies, 29 effect sizes, N = 2,771.
  - Effects were large overall, but the mean **proportion of target words learned was only 9–18% (immediate) and 6–17% (delayed)**.
  - By mode (immediate/delayed): reading 17%/15%, listening 15%/13%, reading-while-listening 13%/17%, viewing 7%/5%.
  - Moderators: proficiency, text type, spacing, mode of input.
  - Source: [Language Teaching](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507)
- **Krashen today [essay, not empirical]** (Lichtman & VanPatten 2021):
  - Monitor Theory was "widely criticized and dismissed", but its ideas persist under new names.
  - The acquisition–learning distinction maps onto implicit vs explicit learning.
  - The natural order hypothesis maps onto ordered development.
  - The input hypothesis maps onto the central role of "communicatively embedded input in all theories of SLA".
  - Source: [Foreign Language Annals](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12552)
- **Shintani, Li & Ellis 2013 [MA, 35 research projects]**:
  - Comprehension-based (CBI) and production-based (PBI) grammar instruction both had large effects on receptive and productive knowledge.
  - CBI's advantage on receptive knowledge within one week diminished at delayed tests, and was largely due to Processing Instruction.
  - For productive knowledge, the two were similar short-term, but **PBI was more effective at delayed tests**.
  - Source: [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12001)
- **Chinese-specific ER (weak evidence)** (Zhou & Day 2023):
  - A 13-week ER program for Chinese L2 at a US liberal-arts college, evaluated only through attitude surveys and reflections.
  - ER "significantly improved" confidence in reading Chinese and devotion to learning Chinese.
  - Students *perceived* gains in fluency, vocabulary, grammar and character recognition.
  - No control group and no proficiency test.
  - Source: [Reading in a Foreign Language](https://hdl.handle.net/10125/67448)

### Inferences
- A graded reading and listening module is well justified. Effects did not depend on program length in Jeon & Day, so a few months of regular reading should already help reading rate and comprehension.
- The larger effect of web-delivered texts (d = 0.89) is encouraging for a mobile format. It is confounded with adult samples, though, and rests on only 10 samples.
- **Coverage control should be a hard requirement.** Texts and audio should be selected or generated so that about 95–98% of tokens (reading) and about 90–95% (listening) are already known. "Known" would come from the learner's HSK level and spaced-repetition history.
- For Chinese, coverage has to be computed on segmented words *and* on characters. No validated Chinese threshold was found (see Gaps).
- Incidental uptake per text is low (9–18%), so input alone will be slow for building the HSK vocabulary. Texts should deliberately recycle words from the learner's deck (see Q5).
- ER principles (self-selection, enjoyment) suggest offering several texts per level and topic. Jeon & Day treat self-selection as a defining feature of real ER.
- The input-only position (strong Krashen) is not supported. Combining input with explicit grammar and production practice fits the meta-analytic evidence better (see Q2 and Q4).

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of ER or graded readers for **Chinese L2** was found; the only CFL program study is perception-based.
- No study was found that establishes **lexical or character coverage thresholds for reading or listening comprehension in Chinese L2**. Word segmentation and the character-versus-word distinction make the English figures only approximate.
- Nakanishi's outcome-level effects (reading rate d ≈ 0.98, comprehension 0.63, vocabulary 0.18) appeared only in a search-engine summary. They were not verified in the primary text.
- The results of the 2021 Hu & Nation replication (104 Sri Lankan adults) were not retrieved.
- Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) and Waring & Takaki (2003, vocabulary uptake from graded readers) were not verified in this session.
- No meta-analysis of extensive *listening* was found (see Q6 for single studies).

## 2. Explicit vs implicit grammar instruction: durability, type of knowledge, implications for short explanations + practice

### Takeaway
L2 instruction has large effects overall (g = 1.06 across 54 studies) that persist at delayed posttests. Explicit instruction outperformed implicit in Norris & Ortega (d = 1.13 vs 0.54), Spada & Tomita, and Goo et al. However:
- outcome measures are skewed toward controlled, explicit-knowledge tests;
- the broadest recent synthesis (Kang, Sok & Han 2019) found only a "minor" difference between explicit and implicit instruction;
- secondary sources report implicit instruction doing better on spontaneous use and on delayed tests.

The best-supported design is short explicit explanations, plus plenty of meaningful practice, plus input.

### Cited Findings
- **Norris & Ortega 2000 [MA, 49 unique samples, 1980–1998]**:
  - Focused instruction gives large target-oriented gains.
  - **Explicit > implicit**, and Focus on Form ≈ Focus on Forms (both large).
  - Effects are durable.
  - The type of outcome measure "likely affects the magnitude of observed instructional effectiveness".
  - Generalisability is limited by weak operationalisation and lack of replication.
  - Source: [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136)
- Norris & Ortega's figures were **implicit d = 0.54 vs explicit d = 1.13**, as reported by Kang, Sok & Han (2019) — [Kang, Sok & Han, LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818776671)
- **Spada & Tomita 2010 [MA, 41 studies, English grammatical features only]**: larger effects for explicit over implicit instruction for **both simple and complex features**. Explicit instruction contributed to both **controlled knowledge and spontaneous use** — [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x)
- **Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella 2015 [MA, 34 samples: 11 from Norris & Ortega plus 23 new studies, 1999–2011]**: explicit instruction was more effective than implicit overall — [Goo et al., John Benjamins](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/books/9789027268723-sibil.48.18goo)
- **Kang, Sok & Han 2019 [MA, 54 studies, 5,051 learners, six major journals, 1980–2015]**:
  - Overall **g = 1.06** (95% CI 0.84–1.29).
  - Only **"a minor difference" between explicit and implicit instruction**.
  - Significant moderators: outcome measure mode, learners' starting proficiency, research setting, and intensity of instruction.
  - Source: [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818776671)
  - The authors call their implicit-instruction finding ("consistent with Goo et al. (2015)") "a major reversal" of Norris & Ortega (2000) — [Kang, Sok & Han](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818776671)
- **[secondary]** characterisations of Goo et al. and Kang et al.:
  - "in spontaneous [L2] use, implicit instruction showed stronger effects (Kang et al. 2019)" — [IRAL 2023](https://doi.org/10.1515/iral-2023-0079)
  - both explicit and implicit instruction produced significant immediate effects, "but the learning effects of implicit instruction lasted for longer" (Goo et al. 2015; Kang et al. 2019) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022, an L2 *Chinese* form-focused instruction study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.790424)
- **[quasi, single study]** 80 Flemish adults learning Hungarian. For *simple* rules, listening activities and comprehensible input had a positive effect at a basic level. Adding explicit rule explanation and practice gave **no extra benefit** — [ITL 2022](https://doi.org/10.1075/itl.21038.ger)
- See also Q1: production-based practice was more effective for **productive** knowledge at delayed tests — [Shintani, Li & Ellis 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12001)

### Inferences
- Short explicit grammar notes (one or two screens with contrastive examples, e.g. 了 vs 过, 的/得/地), followed straight away by practice, are well supported. They are also efficient for self-study adults.
- The explicit advantage in older meta-analyses partly reflects **controlled test formats**. These resemble HSK item types (gap-fill, sentence ordering), so explicit teaching likely transfers well to the *exam*.
- Spontaneous speaking and writing (HSK oral and writing tasks) need meaning-focused practice and input on top of explicit teaching. This is where implicit and interactive learning may matter most.
- Instruction effects are durable, but grammar points should still be scheduled for spaced review (see Q5, Kim & Webb).
- Implicit exposure alone may work for simple, salient patterns (single Hungarian study). It is probably not enough for the low-salience features typical of Chinese, such as aspect particles and complements. This is an extrapolation, not tested.

### Gaps
- Exact explicit and implicit g values in Goo et al. (2015) and Kang et al. (2019), and the immediate/delayed and controlled/free-production breakdowns, were not verified (paywalled).
- No meta-analysis of grammar instruction **in Chinese L2** was found. Almost all primary studies target English or Spanish morphosyntax.
- How Chinese grammar points map onto "simple vs complex" in Spada & Tomita's sense is unknown.

## 3. Corrective feedback (oral, written, automated): which types work, and implications for an app

### Takeaway
Corrective feedback (CF) reliably helps, with medium effects (d ≈ 0.6–0.75) that last over time:
- **prompts** that push self-repair beat **recasts** in classrooms;
- **explicit** CF has bigger immediate effects, while **implicit** CF is better maintained;
- written CF improves accuracy (g ≈ 0.5–0.7);
- automated writing evaluation has g ≈ 0.55–0.59, helping vocabulary more than grammar;
- computer-mediated interaction is about as effective as face-to-face.

For automated feedback this suggests: targeted CF, a self-correction attempt first, then an explicit correction with a short rule.

### Cited Findings
- **Li 2010 [MA, 33 studies: 22 published, 11 PhD dissertations]**:
  - medium overall effect, **maintained over time**;
  - **implicit feedback better maintained than explicit**;
  - lab studies > classroom studies;
  - **shorter treatments > longer**;
  - foreign-language contexts > second-language contexts;
  - published studies no larger than dissertations.
  - Source: [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x)
  - Explicit feedback had larger *immediate* effects — [summary of Li 2010, Auckland repository](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/24985)
  - The overall effect is cited as **d = 0.64** — [SSLA 2023 meta-analysis on note-taking](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263123000529); [Bibauw et al. 2022](https://hdl.handle.net/10125/73488) — and as d = 0.61 and 0.64 — [Language Learning Journal 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2017.1400579) [secondary]
- **Lyster & Saito 2010 [MA, 15 classroom studies, N = 827]**:
  - CF had significant and **durable** effects;
  - **prompts > recasts**;
  - effects were clearest on **free constructed responses**;
  - long treatments > short-to-medium ones;
  - younger learners benefited more than older ones.
  - Source: [SSLA](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990520)
  - Overall effect cited as about **d = 0.74–0.75** — [Language Learning Journal 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2017.1400579); [Plonsky & Brown 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658314536436) [secondary]
- **Brown 2016 [MA of observational classroom studies]**: what teachers actually do. **Recasts make up 57% of CF and prompts 30%**; grammar errors receive the largest share (43%) — [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168814563200)
- **Plonsky & Brown 2015**: different domain definitions and search techniques explain why 18 feedback meta-analyses disagree. Meta-analyses limited to oral and computer-mediated CF found d ≈ 0.64–0.74 — [Second Language Research](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658314536436)
- **Written CF** (Kang & Han 2015 [MA, 21 studies]): written CF improves grammatical accuracy in L2 writing, but its effect depends on proficiency, setting and genre — [Modern Language Journal](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12189). Reported overall **g = 0.68** — [JECR 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/07356331221127300) — and **direct > indirect CF (g = 0.60 vs 0.33)** — [Sustainability 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/su13158192) [secondary]
- **Automated writing evaluation (AWE)**:
  - Ngo, Chen & Lai 2022 [three-level MA]:
    - between-group **g = 0.59** (24 studies, 85 effect sizes); within-group g = 0.98 (34 studies);
    - **more effective for vocabulary than for grammar**;
    - Grammarly looked promising, Pigai did not;
    - short use gave no advantage over non-AWE conditions, while medium-to-long use did;
    - working with peers looked particularly effective.
    - Source: [Interactive Learning Environments](https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2022.2096642)
  - Fleckenstein et al. 2023 [MA]: automated feedback **g = 0.55**, with high heterogeneity and no significant moderator differences — [Frontiers in AI](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2023.1162454)
- **Computer-mediated vs face-to-face interaction** (Ziegler 2016 [MA, k = 14]): both were effective, with **no significant difference between modes**. There was a small advantage for chat on productive and written measures, and for face-to-face on receptive and oral ones — [SSLA](https://doi.org/10.1017/S027226311500025X)
- **Dialogue-based CALL** (Bibauw et al. 2022):
  - providing CF was one of the effective features of dialogue systems;
  - its *relative* effect was lower than Li's d = 0.64, because bot conversations always contain interactional feedback even in "no-CF" conditions.
  - Source: [LLT](https://hdl.handle.net/10125/73488)
- **Human vs ChatGPT feedback** (Steiss et al. 2024; 200 secondary-school essays, native speakers and English learners): human feedback was better on every quality criterion except being criteria-based, *including accuracy*. The authors still judged the practical differences "not substantial" — [Learning and Instruction](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2024.101894)

### Inferences
- **Feedback design for exercises:**
  1. After an error, *prompt* first: flag the location, give a metalinguistic hint, and allow a retry.
  2. Then give the explicit correction plus a one-line rule.

  This combines prompts > recasts with explicit (strong immediately) and implicit, elicitation-type (better maintained) feedback.
- **For speaking with a bot**: use light recasts during conversation, followed by a short explicit summary of the main errors at the end of the task. This keeps the exchange flowing and still makes the errors visible.
- **For written production** (character writing, HSK sentence construction): direct correction is the safer default for beginners, given direct > indirect. This rests on a secondary-source figure.
- **Automated feedback** has medium effects but is weaker on grammar. With Chinese and LLM correctors, accuracy is a real risk (see Q9). Grammar feedback should therefore come from verified rules or answer keys wherever possible.
- **Adults** benefit somewhat less from CF than children (Lyster & Saito), but still clearly benefit.

### Gaps
- The overall Kang & Han figure is inconsistent across secondary sources (0.68 vs about 0.54 cited elsewhere). It was not verified in the primary text.
- No CF meta-analysis specific to **Chinese L2** or to **tone errors** was found.
- Evidence on CF delivered by fully automated systems to adult self-study learners (no teacher) is thin.

## 4. Output and interaction (Swain, Mackey & Goo); task-based language teaching (TBLT)

### Takeaway
Interaction, meaning negotiation plus feedback, has positive and fairly large effects. Text and voice chat are as effective as face-to-face, and production practice matters for productive knowledge at delayed tests. TBLT *programs* show positive effects, but their size is disputed: d = 0.93, cut to g = 0.61 in a re-analysis, and critics say the field is "not ripe" for a meaningful meta-analysis. This supports task-based speaking and writing modules aligned with HSK communicative tasks, with modest expectations.

### Cited Findings
- **Mackey & Goo 2007 [MA, book chapter, Oxford University Press]**: synthesis of interaction research in SLA — [Lancaster record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/59863/). Interaction effects were significantly larger in **lab than classroom** studies — [McGill eScholarship (citing commentary)](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/3r074z95m) [secondary]
- **Ziegler 2016** (see Q3): interaction is effective in both computer-mediated and face-to-face modes, with no significant difference between them — [SSLA](https://doi.org/10.1017/S027226311500025X)
- **Production-based practice** beats comprehension-only practice for **productive knowledge at delayed tests** — [Shintani, Li & Ellis 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12001)
- **Interactive** meaning-focused listening tasks gave more word learning than non-interactive ones (de Vos et al. 2018 [MA]) — [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12296)
- **Bryfonski & McKay 2019 [MA, 52 studies of TBLT *programs*]**:
  - overall **d = 0.93** on a range of outcomes;
  - moderators: program region, institution type, needs analysis, cycles of implementation;
  - **positive stakeholder perceptions**.
  - Source: [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168817744389)
- **Xuan, Cheung & Liu 2022 [technical comment plus re-analysis]**:
  - criticisms of Bryfonski & McKay: loose inclusion criteria, oversimplified effect size calculation, neglected moderators;
  - corrected estimate **g = 0.61**;
  - only sample size and teacher-as-researcher explained heterogeneity;
  - fewer acceptable studies, so "should be interpreted with caution".
  - Source: [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127)
- **Boers, Bryfonski, Faez & McKay 2021**: meta-analytic conclusions depend on how effect sizes are computed, how factors are defined, and which studies are included. Re-running three meta-analyses gave different outcomes — [SSLA](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263120000327)
- **Boers & Faez (LTR, online 2023, 2026 issue)**:
  - re-examined 16 studies from Bryfonski & McKay plus newer studies;
  - found conceptual ambiguity between "task-supported" and "task-based" programs, and unclear definitions of "task";
  - most comparative studies were inadequate;
  - conclusion: "the field is not ripe yet for a meaningful meta-analysis".
  - Sources: [summary/abstract (todoele)](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/meta-analysis-estimate-relative-effectiveness-tblt-programs-are-we-there-yet); [article](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688231167573)
- **Dialogue-based CALL for speaking** (Hou & Min 2025 [MA, 16 studies, 89 effect sizes]): **g = .61** (details in Q9) — [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344025100268)

### Inferences
- The project brief says the HSK syllabus is organised around communicative tasks. That fits a **task cycle** in the app:
  1. pre-task input (model dialogue or text at controlled coverage);
  2. task (role-play with a constrained bot, or writing a message or note);
  3. post-task focus on form (a CF summary plus a short explicit note).

  This combines the interaction, CF and explicit-instruction evidence. Claims of "TBLT efficacy" should use the moderate g ≈ 0.6, not d = 0.93.
- Text chat and voice chat are both legitimate interaction channels (Ziegler). For self-study adults without partners, a bot is the scalable interlocutor (see Q9), and recorded or written "pushed output" with feedback adds output practice.
- Positive learner perceptions of TBLT may help engagement, but perceptions are not outcomes.

### Gaps
- **Mackey & Goo (2007) effect sizes** were not verified. Secondary sources give roughly immediate d ≈ 0.71–0.75 and short-delayed d ≈ 1.09, but the attribution in one listing is ambiguous.
- No primary source on **Swain's output hypothesis** was retrieved. No meta-analysis isolating "pushed output" was found, so the evidence is indirect (production- vs comprehension-based instruction, interaction).
- No TBLT studies on **Chinese L2** or on **app-based self-study** were found.
- The new HSK syllabus task structure was not verified here (other notes cover it).

## 5. Vocabulary: number of encounters, intentional vs incidental learning, context vs lists, chunks, word cards

### Takeaway
- Repetition matters but there is no magic number: the frequency–learning correlation is r = .34.
- Incidental uptake per text is small: 9–18% of target words.
- Deliberate activities (flashcards, word lists, writing, gap-fill) produce about 60% immediate recall, but only about 39% (meaning) and 25% (form) at delayed tests.
- Spacing has medium-to-large effects, and longer gaps are better for long-term retention.

The best design pairs a spaced-retrieval deck that trains both meaning recall and form recall with input that reuses the same words in context.

### Cited Findings
- **Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019 [MA of correlational studies: 26 studies, 45 effect sizes, N = 1,918]**:
  - number of encounters and incidental vocabulary learning correlate at **r = .34** (medium);
  - learner moderators: age, vocabulary knowledge;
  - treatment moderators: spaced learning, visual support, engagement, range in number of encounters;
  - methodological moderators: nonword use, forewarning of a test, test format.
  - Source: [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12343)
- **Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara 2020 [MA of intentional activities: 22 studies, 100 effect sizes]**:
  - activities: flashcards, word lists, writing, fill-in-the-blanks;
  - mean gains **60.1% (meaning recall) and 58.5% (form recall) immediate**, falling to **39.4% and 25.1%** at delayed tests;
  - wide range across activities: **18.4–77.0%** immediate and 23.9–73.4% delayed;
  - **learners' place of study and direction of learning moderated learning**;
  - the authors conclude that learning through word-focused tasks "is far from guaranteed".
  - Source: [Modern Language Journal](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671)
- **Kim & Webb 2022 [MA of spaced practice: 48 experiments, 98 effect sizes, N = 3,411]**:
  - spacing has a **medium-to-large** effect;
  - shorter spacing equals longer spacing at immediate posttests but is **worse at delayed posttests**;
  - **equal and expanding spacing are statistically equivalent**;
  - moderators: learning target, number of sessions, type of practice, activity type, feedback timing, retention interval.
  - Source: [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479)
  - Pooled spacing effect cited as **g = 0.58** — [SSLA 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263123000529) [secondary]
- **de Vos et al. 2018 [MA: 32 studies, 105 effect sizes, N = 1,964]**:
  - incidental word learning from **spoken** meaning-focused input **g = 1.05**;
  - **adults outperformed children**;
  - **interactive** tasks beat non-interactive ones;
  - recognition tests give higher scores than recall tests;
  - studies with no-input control groups showed smaller effects, which protects against overestimation.
  - Source: [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12296)
- Incidental proportions by mode (reading, listening, reading-while-listening about 13–17%; viewing 5–7%): see Q1 — [Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507)

### Inferences
- **Deck design:**
  - Train both **meaning recall** (hanzi/audio → meaning) and **form recall** (meaning → typed or handwritten hanzi plus tone). Form recall decays most (25% delayed), and direction of learning moderates gains.
  - Use **longer spacing** for exam-date retention. Equal and expanding schedules are equivalent, so any reasonable SRS algorithm is acceptable (FSRS, SM-2).
  - Prefer **retrieval with feedback** over passive list review. The 18–77% spread across activities shows that the choice of activity matters.
- **Context:** pure repetition explains only part of learning (r = .34). Graded texts and audio should be generated or selected to re-expose deck words in varied contexts. Spacing, engagement and visual support are listed moderators.
- **Spoken input with interaction** (bot dialogues or listening tasks with questions) is a plausible vocabulary source for adults (g = 1.05, adults > children).
- **Chinese-specific caveat:** "form" for Chinese includes character recognition, writing and tone. All of the evidence above is on alphabetic L2s.

### Gaps
- **Nation's word-card guidelines** (retrieval, spacing, L1 translation, interleaving, keyword mnemonics) were not retrieved in primary form in this session. Other notes on SRS and flashcards may cover them.
- **Collocations and formulaic chunks**: no meta-analytic source was retrieved.
- No Chinese-specific meta-analysis on vocabulary or character learning (radicals, mnemonics, handwriting vs typing) was found here.
- The meta-analyses show correlations and averages only. There is no evidence-based "N encounters" rule (sometimes quoted as 8–12+), and no source was verified for one.

## 6. Listening development: shadowing, dictation, listening strategy instruction, captions, extensive listening

### Takeaway
- **Best supported:** captioned video (g ≈ 0.99 for comprehension), listening strategy instruction (d = 0.69), and reading-while-listening to graded readers followed by post-listening activities.
- **Shadowing:** small Japanese-EFL studies plus two 2025 systematic reviews. It is plausible for bottom-up decoding (phoneme and word recognition) and for prosody and fluency, especially for lower-proficiency learners. There is no meta-analysis.
- **Dictation:** no synthesis was found.
- **Chinese:** tone perception (especially Tones 2 and 3) is a specific weak point for L2 listeners.

### Cited Findings
- **Listening strategy instruction** (Dalman & Plonsky 2022 [MA, 45 studies, 51 samples]): **d = 0.69** (medium), varying with moderators — [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688211072981)
- **Captioned video** (Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet 2013 [MA]): large effects of captions on listening comprehension, **g = 0.99** (95% CI 0.60–1.38, k = 15), and on vocabulary, **g = 0.87** — [System](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2013.07.013); figures as reported in [Noetel et al. 2022, RER](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329) and [Language Learning Journal 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2019.1615978)
- **Incidental vocabulary from viewing is smaller** (7% immediate, 5% delayed) than from listening (15%, 13%) — [Webb et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507)
- **Extensive listening with graded readers:**
  - Chang & Millett 2014: 13 weeks, 10 Level-1 audio graded readers. The **reading-while-listening** group produced the most consistent and significant gains in listening fluency, compared with reading-only and listening-only groups — [ELT Journal](https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/cct052)
  - Chang & Millett 2016: 76 EFL university students, 15 graded readers over 15 weeks. Groups doing 5, 10 or 15 post-listening activities showed **very small, medium and very large** improvement effects respectively — [RELC Journal](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688216631175)
- **Reading-while-listening (RWL) vs reading only** (Clinton-Lisell [preprint MA, 30 studies, N = 1,945]):
  - overall comprehension benefit **g = .18** (trivial);
  - g = .41 only when reading was **experimenter-paced**; self-paced g = .06 (n.s.);
  - possible benefits for L2 incidental vocabulary, but too few studies.
  - Source: [OSF preprint](https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/ku6tv)
- **Shadowing:**
  - Hamada 2016 [quasi, 43 Japanese EFL university students, 9 lessons]: phoneme perception (dictation-cloze test) improved in both low- and intermediate-proficiency groups. **Only low-proficiency learners improved on the listening comprehension items** — [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815597504)
  - Whitworth & Rose 2025 [SR, 44 studies, narrative synthesis; pronunciation]:
    - shadowing can improve **comprehensibility, intelligibility, accentedness, fluency and prosody**;
    - effects on **segmentals were inconclusive**;
    - learners find it enjoyable;
    - methodological problems: over-reliance on controlled tasks, acoustic measures not linked to listener judgments, teacher-collected surveys.
    - Source: [Research Synthesis in Applied Linguistics](https://doi.org/10.1080/29984475.2025.2546827)
  - El Moussaoui 2025 [SR, lower-tier journal]: shadowing helped beginners and lower-proficiency EFL learners perceive sounds and recognise words in connected speech. Advanced learners benefited less, and long-term retention is unknown — [IJLLS](https://doi.org/10.36892/ijlls.v7i4.2260)
  - Hamada 2021 (practitioner article): shadowing has been popular in Japan for about 20 years; describes lesson procedures for listening and pronunciation — [The Language Teacher](https://doi.org/10.37546/jalttlt45.6-3)
- **Mandarin-specific listening:**
  - Japanese learners of Mandarin at elementary and intermediate level drew on both **semantic context and F0 (pitch) contours**. Context mattered more when pitch information was degraded, and was used less efficiently in noise — [Frontiers in Psychology 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00908)
  - Thai L2 learners identified tones less accurately than native speakers, **especially Tone 3, with Tone 2/Tone 3 confusions** — [Behavioral Sciences 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15081062)

### Inferences
- **Listening module:**
  - graded audio with switchable **hanzi and pinyin captions** (synchronised highlighting);
  - **paced** reading-while-listening (the RWL benefit shows up mainly when pace is controlled), then a listening-only replay;
  - several **post-listening tasks** per text (Chang & Millett 2016);
  - brief **strategy training**: predicting, monitoring, using context (d = 0.69).
- **Shadowing**: offer short sentence-level drills, mainly for beginners and low-proficiency users, ideally with pitch or ASR feedback (see Q9). The evidence is weak but the cost is low.
- **Dictation and partial dictation** are better framed as *diagnostics* of bottom-up decoding: Hamada used a dictation cloze as the outcome measure. Treat them as low-cost practice with unproven added value.
- **Tone perception practice** (minimal pairs, Tone 2/Tone 3 contrasts, tones in sentence context) is a plausible Chinese-specific need. The training-efficacy evidence (high-variability phonetic training) was not retrieved here.

### Gaps
- No **meta-analysis of shadowing** was found. The studies are mostly Japanese EFL, and no Chinese-L2 shadowing trial was found.
- No synthesis on **dictation** as a learning technique was found in this session.
- **High-variability phonetic training for Mandarin tones** (e.g. Wang et al. 1999) and perception-training meta-analyses were not verified here.
- No meta-analysis of **extensive listening** was found.

## 7. Mobile-assisted language learning (MALL); Duolingo and other app efficacy studies; what apps lack

### Takeaway
MALL meta-analyses report large pooled effects (g ≈ 0.72–0.88), but the underlying studies are short, small and at high risk of bias. Only 84 of about 1,144 experimental MALL references met minimal standards in one comprehensive review.

Duolingo's evidence base is weak:
- one company-commissioned report (2012);
- one tiny independent case study (n = 9);
- company-authored proficiency studies covering reading and listening only;
- no randomised controlled comparison.

In the independent studies of Duolingo and Babbel, gains track time on task. What apps typically lack in this literature: validated speaking and interaction, extended input, satisfying materials, and rigorous long-term evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- **Burston 2015 [review of MALL learning outcomes]**:
  - Of 291 MALL studies, only 35 met minimal conditions: at least 10 experimental subjects over at least a month.
  - 16 of those had serious design flaws, leaving **19 reliable studies**.
  - 15 of the 19 reported unequivocal positive results; all studies on reading, listening and speaking favoured MALL.
  - The remaining four, all on vocabulary, did not show an unequivocal advantage.
  - Source: [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344014000159)
- **Burston & Giannakou 2022 [MA, 1994–2019]**: of 1,144 references (700 with quantitative outcomes), **only 84 studies met inclusion criteria**. Effect sizes were 0.72 between-group and 1.16 within-group — [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344021000240)
- **Mihaylova et al. 2022 [MA, 23 effect sizes, 2007–2019]**: mobile apps vs traditional instruction **g = 0.88**, but "**high risk of bias and low quality of evidence across all included studies**" — [Psychologica Belgica](https://doi.org/10.5334/pb.1146)
- Taj et al. 2016 [MA, 13 EFL studies, low-tier journal]: d = 0.8 — [ALLS](https://doi.org/10.7575/aiac.alls.v.7n.2p.76)
- **Vesselinov & Grego 2012 [company-commissioned]**:
  - commissioned by Duolingo; data collection and analysis done by independent researchers;
  - main claim: **34 hours ≈ the first college semester of Spanish** (WebCAPE test);
  - 8-week study; average gain about 91 points, about 8 points per hour;
  - substantial attrition: many participants "dropped out or only studied for two hours";
  - travel-motivated learners gained 17.6 points per hour vs 5.7 for others; more effective for beginners.
  - Source: [TechCrunch summary](https://techcrunch.com/2013/01/17/study-learning-spanish-with-duolingo-can-be-more-effective-than-college-classes-or-rosetta-stone/)
  - Krashen's critique: the "only published study … was funded by Duolingo", and only 90 of 156 volunteers completed the 30 hours — [Krashen letter](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/research_on_duolingo.pdf)
- **Loewen et al. 2019 [case study, n = 9, ab initio Turkish, about a semester]**:
  - gains on L2 measures;
  - **moderate positive correlation between time on Duolingo and gains**;
  - flexibility and gamification viewed positively, but **variable motivation and frustration with the instructional materials**;
  - notes that independent research had already reported problems with learner persistence and efficacy.
  - Source: [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344019000065)
- **Loewen, Isbell & Sporn 2020 (Babbel) [pre–post, no control, n = 54, Spanish, 12 weeks, about 10 min/day]**: gains in grammar and vocabulary knowledge and in **oral communicative ability**. **Study time was the strongest predictor** of all three measures, and motivation predicted oral growth — [Foreign Language Annals](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12454)
- **Jiang, Rollinson, Plonsky, Gustafson & Pająk 2021 [company-authored: 4 of 5 authors at Duolingo, Plonsky at NAU; cross-sectional, no control group]**:
  - n = 225 US adults who used *only* Duolingo and finished beginning Spanish or French;
  - reached **Intermediate Low in reading and Novice High in listening** (ACTFL);
  - "comparable" to university students at the end of the 4th semester;
  - "No other skills were assessed".
  - Source: [Foreign Language Annals](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12600) (affiliations as listed in the article metadata)
  - Duolingo's own blog [company] reports a similar study of the English course for Spanish speakers: 263 learners from end of A1 to end of A2, reaching Intermediate High reading and Intermediate Mid listening — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-well-does-duolingo-teach-english/)
- **Shortt et al. 2023 [SR of 35 Duolingo studies, 2012–2020]**: research was mostly **design-focused, quantitative and based on non-probability samples**; English and the USA dominate; the field emphasises tool creation "rather than the process and outcomes of language learning" — [CALL](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2021.1933540)

### Inferences
- The meta-analyses support "apps can help", not any particular design. The app should **measure its own efficacy**: pre/post HSK-style mock tests, retention cohorts, and ideally A/B tests on learning outcomes rather than just engagement.
- **Time on task predicts gains** (Loewen 2019, 2020). Engagement design matters, but it must channel time into the high-value activities from Q1–Q6, not into easy streak-keeping taps.
- **Gaps in typical apps that this app could fill**, based on these sources:
  - speaking and interaction outcomes (Duolingo's proficiency studies assessed only reading and listening);
  - extended graded input;
  - materials that do not frustrate learners;
  - explicit exam alignment.

### Gaps
- No **independent randomised trial of Duolingo** was found, and **no efficacy study of any Chinese-learning app** (e.g. HelloChinese, Duolingo Chinese) for adults was found.
- **Babbel's funding** of Loewen et al. 2020 was not verified in this session.
- A search summary attributed figures to the Vesselinov & Grego report: mean vs median gain (about 8.1 vs 3.9 points per hour), participant average age 35, 69% college graduates. These were not verified. The original report URL (static.duolingo.com) did not resolve here.
- No peer-reviewed Duolingo **speaking**-outcome study was verified.

## 8. Gamification and motivation: meta-analyses, streaks and leaderboards, overjustification, L2 Motivational Self System, goal-setting, habit formation, self-determination theory

### Takeaway
- **Learning:** gamification has a small-to-medium positive effect on cognitive learning (g = .49), which holds up in rigorous studies.
- **Motivation and behaviour:** effects are smaller and fragile (g = .36 and .25, unstable in rigorous studies).
- **Documented harms:** lower intrinsic motivation and lower exam scores over a semester; points and leaderboards raising quantity without quality; expected tangible rewards undermining intrinsic motivation; leaderboard-related anxiety.
- **Streaks and reminders:** they increase usage (large RCT in children's maths; company data), but rigid streaks can become loss-aversion pressure.
- **Habits:** they take weeks to months (18–254 days), and missing a day does not derail them. If-then plans help (d ≈ 0.65).
- **Ideal L2 self:** predicts *effort* (r = .61) far more than *achievement* (r = .20).

### Cited Findings
- **Sailer & Homner 2020 [MA, 38 publications, 40 experiments]**:
  - cognitive outcomes **g = .49** (95% CI .30–.69; k = 19, N = 1,686);
  - motivational **g = .36** (CI .18–.54; k = 16, N = 2,246);
  - behavioural **g = .25** (CI .04–.46; k = 9, N = 951);
  - **only the cognitive effect was stable in high-rigour studies**;
  - **game fiction and social interaction** moderated behavioural outcomes, and **competition combined with collaboration** was particularly effective.
  - Source: [Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- **Bai, Hew & Huang 2020 [MA plus qualitative synthesis]**: g = 0.504 across 30 studies — [Bai et al., Educational Research Review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2020.100322); figure as reported by [Behavioral Sciences 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071247) [secondary]. A citing paper states that Bai et al. note **performance anxiety from leaderboards and public competition** — [Instructional Science 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-026-09780-9) [secondary]
- **Digital game-based language learning** (Dixon et al. 2022 [MA]): between-group d = 0.50, within-group d = 0.95. Entertainment games beat games designed for L2 education, though the confidence intervals overlap — [LLT](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/handle/10125/73464)
- **Gamification in dialogue-based CALL** (Bibauw et al. 2022 [MA]): gamification features were among the effective design features — [LLT](https://hdl.handle.net/10125/73488)
- **Hanus & Fox 2015 [longitudinal quasi-experiment, a semester-long course with badges, coins and a leaderboard]**: students in the gamified course showed **lower intrinsic motivation and lower final exam scores** than the non-gamified class. The effect on exam scores was mediated by intrinsic motivation — [Computers & Education](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019)
- **Mekler et al. 2017**: points, levels and leaderboards **increased performance quantity without affecting intrinsic motivation** or perceived competence — [Computers in Human Behavior](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048)
- **Kwon & Özpolat 2020** (gamified assessment in an undergraduate course): **significantly lower content knowledge, satisfaction and course experience** — [INFORMS Transactions on Education](https://doi.org/10.1287/ited.2019.0227)
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 [MA, 128 studies] (overjustification)**:
  - engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (**d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28**);
  - engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also reduced self-reported interest (d = −0.15, −0.17);
  - **positive feedback enhanced** free-choice behaviour (d = +0.33) and interest (+0.31);
  - tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than for college students.
  - Sources: [Psychological Bulletin (PDF)](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)
- **Streaks, RCT** (Cristia et al. 2024, Inter-American Development Bank working paper; about 60,000 Peruvian pupils in grades 4–6 using an online maths platform):
  - **streak-highlighting messages** and personalised reminders increased platform use vs generic reminders and control;
  - streaks worked more on the intensive margin (how much active users practised), reminders on the extensive margin (whether pupils used the platform at all);
  - streak messages also **improved maths achievement** vs control.
  - Source: [IDB](https://doi.org/10.18235/0012912)
- **Streaks, company data [company, observational/A-B]** (Duolingo):
  - learners who reach a 7-day streak are "3.6 times more likely to complete their course" (correlational, so self-selection is likely);
  - streak animations increased 7-day retention of new learners by +1.7%;
  - doubling Streak Freezes increased daily active learners by +0.38%;
  - Duolingo describes long streaks as sustained by **loss aversion**.
  - Source: [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- **L2 Motivational Self System** (Al-Hoorie 2018 [MA, 32 reports, 39 samples, N = 32,078]):
  - ideal L2 self, ought-to L2 self and L2 learning experience predict **intended effort** at r = .61, .38 and .41;
  - but **objective achievement** only at r = .20, −.05 and .17;
  - heterogeneity is substantial, and the effort–experience link is partly a scale-wording artifact.
  - Source: [SSLLT](https://doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2018.8.4.2)
- **Self-determination theory in L2** (Al-Hoorie, Oga-Baldwin, Hiver & Vitta [SR, k = 111, three decades]): some mini-theories are well represented in L2 research and others are under-represented or absent — [LTR](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221102686); [postprint](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/InPress_Al-HoorieEtAl_Self-DeterminationMini-Theories.pdf)
- **Habit formation** (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010):
  - 96 volunteers repeated a behaviour daily in a fixed context (e.g. "after breakfast") for 12 weeks;
  - 82 provided enough data, and the model fitted 62 participants;
  - time to reach 95% of maximum automaticity ranged from **18 to 254 days**;
  - "**Missing one opportunity … did not materially affect** the habit formation process".
  - Source: [European Journal of Social Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
  - The popular **66-day** figure is the average from this study — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)
- **Implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 [MA, 94 studies, >8,000 participants]): if-then plans improve goal attainment vs goal intentions alone, **d = 0.65** — [Advances in Experimental Social Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2601(06)38002-1); d as reported in [British Journal of Health Psychology 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.70046) [secondary]

### Inferences
- **Gamification that supports competence and autonomy:**
  - visible progress toward an HSK target level and HSK-like mastery checks;
  - narrative or game fiction;
  - cooperative or social features.

  Avoid public leaderboards by default (make them opt-in, or use small cohorts, collaborative goals or "friends" challenges). Never make tangible or expected rewards the point. Positive, informational feedback is the reward that *raises* intrinsic motivation.
- **Streaks:** keep them, as they likely increase practice, but make them forgiving: streak freezes, weekly targets, and "missed a day is fine" messaging consistent with Lally. Reward *quality* minutes (input, output, retrieval) rather than taps, to avoid quantity-without-quality.
- **Onboarding:**
  - prompt an **if-then plan** ("after dinner → 10 minutes of Chinese");
  - send personalised reminders;
  - set expectations that a habit takes about 2–3 months (or more).
- **Ideal-L2-self exercises** (e.g. "you at HSK 4, ordering in Beijing") may raise effort. Effort-to-achievement transfer is weak (r = .20), so results still have to come from practice quality.
- **Adults** are less harmed than children by tangible rewards (Deci et al.). The documented harms of gamification in higher education (Hanus & Fox; Kwon & Özpolat) still apply.

### Gaps
- No **language-specific meta-analysis of gamification** (as distinct from games) was verified:
  - one aggregator claims none exists;
  - Bai et al.'s language subgroup figure (about g = 0.38) is unverified.
- No peer-reviewed study of **Duolingo-style streaks on adult language-learning outcomes** was found. "Streak anxiety" evidence is anecdotal.
- The abstract of Silverman & Barasch (*Journal of Consumer Research*, on how broken streaks affect later behaviour) was not retrieved: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029).
- Overjustification evidence comes mostly from general-psychology lab tasks, not from L2 apps.
- A further 2024 gamification meta-analysis (Zeng et al., g = 0.782 across 22 experimental studies) is cited secondarily in the 2026 Behavioral Sciences paper and was not reviewed.

## 9. AI and speech technology: LLM chatbots, ASR and computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT), accuracy limits for Mandarin and non-native speakers, risks

### Takeaway
- **Chatbot effects:** meta-analyses of chatbots, both pre-LLM and generative-AI, converge on **medium effects** (d/g ≈ 0.58–0.61). But the primary studies are mostly short, small, EFL and based in Asia, and novelty effects are documented.
- **A retraction:** a widely cited ChatGPT meta-analysis reporting g = 0.867 was **retracted** in 2026.
- **ASR and CAPT** pronunciation training gives medium effects (g ≈ 0.69; d ≈ 0.68). Effects are large for segmentals but **small for suprasegmentals**, which matters because lexical tones are prosodic. They also appear only with medium-to-long use and **explicit** feedback, and are small when learners practise alone.
- **Reliability:** ASR recognition of L2 speech does not reliably mirror human intelligibility, and LLMs **over-correct and hallucinate** on learner Chinese.

AI is best used for practice volume, scaffolding and conversation, with guardrails, verified content and calibrated feedback.

### Cited Findings
**LLM and chatbot meta-analyses and reviews**
- **Li (Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 2025) [MA, 41 experimental or quasi-experimental studies from 2023 on, 48 effect sizes, N = 3,515]**:
  - generative-AI chatbots had a "moderate to large" effect on second language acquisition, **ES = 0.576** (95% CI 0.385–0.768);
  - no differences by educational stage or learning environment;
  - large moderating effects for L1 Indonesian, **L2 other than English**, ChatGPT, vocabulary targets, and **1–7-day interventions**.
  - Source: [JCAL](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.70060)
- **Lyu (International Journal of Applied Linguistics, 2024–25) [MA, 31 studies, 41 effect sizes]**: chatbots have a medium effect on L2 learning, **g = 0.608**. The effect was moderated by mobile access, chatbot modality, **whether generative AI was involved**, and the type of comparison group — [IJAL](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijal.12668)
- **Bibauw, Van den Noortgate, François & Desmet 2022 [MA of dialogue-based CALL, 17 studies, k = 100, mostly pre-LLM]**:
  - **d = 0.58** on L2 proficiency;
  - effective features: form-focused and goal-oriented systems, system-guided interactions, corrective feedback and gamification;
  - effects were established **for lower-proficiency learners** and for vocabulary, morphosyntax, holistic proficiency and accuracy.
  - Source: [LLT](https://hdl.handle.net/10125/73488)
- **Hou & Min 2025 [three-level MA, 16 studies, 89 effect sizes]**: dialogue systems improved **L2 speaking**, **g = .61**. Significant moderators were type of system, system meaning constraint and modality. Corrective feedback, proficiency, intervention length and interaction type were *not* significant — [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344025100268)
- **Wu & Yu 2023 [MA, 24 randomised studies, general education]**: AI chatbots had a large effect on learning outcomes, larger in higher education. **Short interventions had stronger effects than long ones**, which the authors attribute to **novelty effects** — [BJET](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13334)
- **Huang, Hew & Fryer 2022 [SR, 25 studies, chatbots for language learning]**:
  - affordances: timeliness, ease of use, personalisation;
  - pedagogical uses: interlocutor, simulation, transmission, helpline, recommendations;
  - challenges: **technological limitations, the novelty effect, and cognitive load**.
  - Source: [JCAL](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12610)
- **Voice-based AI chatbots** (qualitative meta-synthesis of 57 studies, 2010–2024, English learning): combining ASR and text-to-speech enables natural spoken conversation practice — [ReCALL 2024 (PDF)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/118654390007CCA33DEAD142D45C8D4F/S0958344024000168a.pdf/use_of_artificially_intelligent_chatbots_in_english_language_learning_a_systematic_metasynthesis_study_of_articles_published_between_2010_and_2024.pdf)
- **Retraction warning**: Wang & Fan's 2025 meta-analysis (51 studies; ChatGPT on learning performance **g = 0.867**) was **retracted** — [retracted article](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-04787-y); [retraction note 2026](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-07310-z)
- Deng et al. 2025 (*Computers & Education*, ChatGPT and student learning in general education) is reported as giving a pooled g = 0.74 — [Deng et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224); figure per [Frontiers in Psychology 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1811130) [secondary]
- Bibauw 2025 (conference keynote, preliminary): a new meta-analysis spanning the pre- and post-ChatGPT "eras". The field is moving from comparing technologies to comparing pedagogical conditions. Bibauw calls for attention to **task design and scaffolding, especially productive support for less proficient speakers** — [UCLouvain](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/handle/2078.5/248944)

**ASR, CAPT and pronunciation**
- **Ngo, Chen & Lai (ReCALL, online 2023) [MA, 15 studies, 38 effect sizes, 2008–2021, ESL/EFL]**:
  - ASR overall **g = 0.69**;
  - ASR with **explicit CF** is largely effective, while indirect feedback (ASR dictation) is moderately effective;
  - **large effect on segmentals, small on suprasegmentals**;
  - short use showed **no advantage** over non-ASR conditions, while medium-to-long use did;
  - **practising with peers gave a large effect, practising alone a small one**;
  - large effect for adults and intermediate learners.
  - Source: [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344023000113)
- **Mahdi & Al Khateeb 2019 [MA, 20 studies, N = 1,014]**:
  - computer-assisted pronunciation training **d = 0.68**;
  - equally effective for young and adult learners; more effective for beginner and intermediate than advanced learners;
  - but the studies were "**all of very low quality**" (mostly fewer than 100 participants, single institution, intervention-specific tests), so the evidence is "indicative and not conclusive".
  - Source: [Review of Education](https://doi.org/10.1002/rev3.3165)
- **Pronunciation instruction in general:**
  - Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015 [MA, 86 reports]: d = 0.89 within-group and 0.80 between-group. Effects were larger with **longer interventions, feedback, and controlled outcome measures** — [Applied Linguistics](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amu040)
  - Saito & Plonsky 2019 [MA plus measurement framework, 77 studies]: instruction is most effective on *monitored* production of specific features. Its effect on global, spontaneous speech judged by humans remains unclear — [Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12345)
- **Mandarin tones with CAPT:**
  - Wu, Su & Yin 2025 [RCT, small, 2 weeks]: elementary Mandarin learners at an international university in China were randomised to ASR-based tone CAPT or listen-and-repeat. The CAPT group made **significant tonal-accuracy gains on all four tones at sentence level**; the control group did not. Usability ratings were positive — [International Journal of Chinese Linguistics](https://doi.org/10.1075/ijchl.00040.wu)
  - Chun, Jiang, Meyr & Yang 2015 [pre–post, 35 learners, 9 weeks at 20–25 min/week]: learners compared their own **pitch curves** with native speakers'. Native-speaker ratings showed tone improvement, acoustic analyses showed improvement on some tones, and two-thirds of learners found the pitch curves helpful — [Journal of Second Language Pronunciation](https://doi.org/10.1075/jslp.1.1.04chu)
- **ASR accuracy on L2 speech:**
  - Inceoglu, Chen & Lim 2022 [4 Taiwanese intermediate English learners, 12 L1 listeners, Google ASR]:
    - intelligibility on words: ASR 40.81% vs L1 listeners 38.62%; on sentences: ASR 75.52% vs listeners 83.88%;
    - error types were similar;
    - but ASR mirrored listener judgments for **only one speaker** (plus one more per task), so ASR's agreement with listeners "may depend on individual speakers".
    - Source: [ReCALL](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344022000192)
  - Shimanskaya 2025 [15 L2 French learners]: ASR transcription was **less accurate than human transcribers**, though ASR accuracy, human transcription and teacher intelligibility ratings were correlated — [Foreign Language Annals](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12807)
  - McGuire 2025 [preprint, L2 English, L2-ARCTIC corpus]: on *read* speech, Whisper and AssemblyAI had mean match error rates of 0.054–0.056, "approaching human-level"; on spontaneous speech the best system (RevAI) reached 0.063. Systems varied considerably in handling disfluencies — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06924)

**LLM correction of learner Chinese**
- **Lin 2024** (state-of-the-art LLMs on Chinese grammatical error correction, learner corpora):
  - LLMs **over-correct even when explicitly prompted for "minimal edits"**, rewriting sentences into fluent ones;
  - they show **under-correction and hallucination in reasoning-dependent cases**;
  - scores were higher on test sets with multiple annotators than with a single one.
  - Source: [PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0312881)
- **[preprints]** Qu & Wu (2023) report that LLMs fall short of earlier state-of-the-art Chinese grammatical error correction systems on automatic metrics, because of over-correction — [arXiv 2307.03972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03972). Fang et al. (2023) report that ChatGPT corrects English fluently but over-corrects and does not follow minimal edits — [arXiv 2304.01746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01746)

### Inferences
- **Chatbot conversation practice is worth including**, as the evidence converges on about 0.6. It should be **system-guided, goal-oriented and form-focused**:
  - role-plays tied to HSK tasks and constrained to the learner's HSK vocabulary;
  - with corrective feedback and gamified goals (the effective features in Bibauw et al.);
  - with scaffolds for HSK 1–2 beginners: bilingual hints, pinyin, suggested replies.

  Expect novelty-driven early gains to fade. Measure long-term effects instead of trusting short-study effect sizes.
- **Guardrails for LLM feedback:**
  - constrain corrections to minimal edits;
  - cross-check with deterministic resources (curated answer keys, grammar-point rules, HSK word lists);
  - show uncertainty, and avoid "correcting" correct answers;
  - never let an unverified LLM judgement decide exam-critical answers (e.g. HSK mock-test scoring).
- **Pronunciation and tones:**
  - The ASR meta-analyses show *small* effects on suprasegmentals and *small* effects when practising **alone**, which is exactly the self-study use case. Plain "speech recognised / not recognised" feedback is therefore unlikely to be enough.
  - Better: **explicit, tone-specific feedback** (which syllable, which tone produced) with **pitch-contour visualisation** (Chun et al.), practice over weeks rather than days, and possibly peer or tutor review.
- **ASR transcripts are not reliable proxies for intelligibility to human listeners**, especially for individual speakers. Treat ASR scores as noisy signals: aggregate over many attempts, and avoid punitive pass/fail gating.
- **Data-protection and cost issues for voice/LLM features** were not researched here.

### Gaps
- No **independent benchmark** was found for current ASR systems (Whisper, Azure, Google, iFlytek, etc.) on **beginner L2 Mandarin speech or tone errors**. The English data above concern read speech by fairly proficient speakers, and Wu et al. 2025 is one small two-week study.
- None of the ASR meta-analyses covered Mandarin. Ngo et al. covered only ESL/EFL learners, and Mahdi & Al Khateeb covered various languages with very low-quality studies.
- The generative-AI meta-analyses mostly include short studies from 2023–2025. A 2026 meta-analysis of 35 experimental ChatGPT studies was found but not reviewed: [HSSC 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07019-z).
- No study of LLM tutors for **Italian-L1 learners of Chinese**, or of LLM-generated graded readers for Chinese, was found.
- Some Hou & Min moderator directions (e.g. effect of "system meaning constraint") could not be read from the abstract.
