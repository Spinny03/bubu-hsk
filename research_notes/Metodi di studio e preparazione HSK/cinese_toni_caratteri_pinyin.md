# Learning Mandarin as a foreign language (adult learners, non-tonal alphabetic L1 / Italian): tones, characters, pinyin and reading. Evidence notes for an HSK-prep app

_Compiled 19 Oct 2026. Evidence tags: [META] = meta-analysis; [EXP] = controlled experiment; [CORPUS] = large learner corpus; [SMALL] = single small study (roughly n < 30), thesis, preprint or pilot; [SECONDARY] = blog, press, encyclopedia or vendor page. Method note: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through. After that, sources were checked through Europe PMC, DOI resolution and publisher/repository pages. CNKI and Baidu Scholar returned 403, so coverage of Chinese-language literature (声调教学, 汉字教学) is thin. Notes are in English; Italian-specific implications are flagged as such._

## 1. Tone perception training (HVPT, number of sessions/trials, multi-talker stimuli, feedback, generalization, retention)

### Takeaway
Identification training with natural words from several native talkers reliably improves L2 tone perception. For Mandarin tones the meta-analytic effect is about g = 1.0. Gains carry over to new words and talkers, last for months, and partly transfer to production. About 400 minutes of total training is the meta-analytic optimum. The extra benefit of *many* talkers over few is small, uncertain and depends on the learner's aptitude, so the best-supported design is adaptive: start with low or blocked variability, increase it as the learner improves, and give immediate, minimal trial-by-trial feedback.

### Cited Findings
**Classic studies**
- Wang, Spence, Jongman & Sereno (1999, JASA). 8 American learners trained in 8 sessions over 2 weeks to identify the four tones in natural words from several native talkers (high-variability paradigm). Identification rose 21% from pretest to post-test. It generalized to new stimuli (+18%) and to new talkers plus new stimuli (+25%). At 6 months the gain was still +21% over pretest. [EXP, n = 8] — [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/499786a0-dec8-4422-aaf9-68909b608369)
- Wang, Jongman & Sereno (2003, JASA). Same trainees, perception-only training. Their post-training tone productions were identified by native listeners 18% more accurately than before. Their F0 contours moved closer to native norms. **Pitch height was more resistant to improvement than pitch contour.** — [doi:10.1121/1.1531176](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1531176)

**Meta-analyses**
- Sakai & Moorman (2018, Applied Psycholinguistics), 25 years of perception-training studies that also tested production. Perception improved with d = 0.92 and production with d = 0.54. The correlation between perception gains and production gains was small to medium and not significant. [META] — [doi:10.1017/S0142716417000418](https://www.doi.org/10.1017/S0142716417000418)
- Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2025, SSLA 47(3)), 79 HVPT studies [META] — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-metaanalysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6):
  - Overall effects: pre/post g = 0.92 (k = 96); treatment vs control g = 0.67 (k = 32). Mean perceptual gain was 14.12% on trained stimuli and 12.96% on untrained stimuli.
  - Retention: almost no decline at delayed post-test (g = −0.08).
  - Generalization: only small differences between novel and trained stimuli (g −0.25 to −0.17).
  - Moderators:
    - task: identification (g = 0.95) beat discrimination (g = 0.57);
    - response labels: keywords/symbols (g = 0.90–1.03) beat pictures (g = 0.47);
    - **total training time: optimum about 400 minutes, with diminishing returns beyond that;**
    - **number of talkers mattered only for higher-proficiency learners;**
    - more prior L2 study meant smaller gains.
  - **Mandarin tones: large effect (g = 1.00), from 12 studies.**
- Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2024, Applied Psycholinguistics), 31 studies of perception-based HVPT measured on production. Effects were small to medium. Mean production gains were 10.50% on trained items and 4.50% on untrained items. [META] — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38D8F5CE65DC708137B0E95F97C6BC7)
- Zhang, Cheng & Zhang (2021, JSLHR), high vs low talker variability, 18 studies, 549 participants [META] — [UMN Experts](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-role-of-talker-variability-in-nonnative-phonetic-learning-a-s/):
  - immediate training outcomes g = 0.46, **shrinking to g = −0.04 after correction for publication bias and outliers**;
  - immediate perceptual gains g = 0.56; production gains not significant;
  - generalization to new talkers g = 0.72; long-term retention g = 1.09;
  - moderators: length of the training program and how talkers were presented (format).

**Talker variability and aptitude (conflicting evidence)**
- Perrachione, Lee, Ha & Wong (2011, JASA). High-variability training helped only learners with strong perceptual abilities. Weaker learners were *impaired* by it compared with low-variability training. The most harmful feature was trial-by-trial variability. — [doi:10.1121/1.3593366](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3593366)
- Sadakata & McQueen (2014, Frontiers). 45 Dutch listeners, 5-day training on bisyllabic Mandarin pseudowords with tone combinations, with 1, 3 or 5 speakers. Variability had no overall effect. It hindered low-aptitude listeners and helped high-aptitude ones. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01318](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01318)
- Dong, Clayards, Brown & Wonnacott (2019, PeerJ). 60 English speakers, 6 training sessions of 288 trials each, with feedback. Conditions: 1 talker; 4 talkers mixed; 4 talkers blocked. All groups improved in perception and production, with transfer to untrained voices and items. There was **no benefit of high variability on any generalization test**; the low-variability group was better on the trained stimuli. Aptitude predicted performance but did not interact with variability. — [PMC6690337](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6690337)
- Nagle, Bruun & Zárate-Sández (2025, Applied Psycholinguistics). Spanish stop consonants, not tones; 342 learners analysed; 2-talker vs 6-talker training, 6 sessions of 120 trials. Both beat the control group and did not differ from each other. The number of sessions completed moderated gains. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/comparing-lower-and-higher-variability-multitalker-perceptual-training/2755FEDEFB74EC4A3DD83011015BCB97)

**Feedback and visual response cues**
- Chandrasekaran, Yi & Maddox (2014, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review). English speakers learned Mandarin tone categories over 480 trials with trial-by-trial feedback and multiple talkers. Feedback that targets implicit ("reflexive") learning, i.e. **immediate and minimally informative**, improved category learning more than delayed or rich feedback. Experiment 3 compared mixed vs blocked talkers; mixed presentation was hypothesized to favour reflexive learning. — [PMC3874422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3874422)
- Godfroid, Lin & Ryu (2017, Language Learning). **303 true beginners (English L1), 3-week web-based training.** Five visual cue conditions: numbers, pitch contours, colours, colour + number, colour + contour. Multimodal training helped tone perception, with a **small practical advantage for pitch contours and numbers over colour**. Two visual cues together were not better than one. [EXP, large n] — [doi:10.1111/lang.12246](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12246); summary also at [HKU Hub](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/244254)
- Liu, Wang, Perfetti, Brubaker, Wu & MacWhinney (2011, Language Learning). In-class study with first-year US university students, tones of 228 syllables across 8 lessons. Conditions: Contour + Pinyin, Number + Pinyin, Contour only. Contour + Pinyin produced more error reduction than Contour only and a larger pre/post gain than Number + Pinyin. The authors conclude that tone and segments each benefit from their own visual support. — [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00673.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00673.x)
- Jiang et al. (2026, European Journal of Neuroscience). 9 Finnish adults, 4 days, each with 25 min of passive and 45 min of active listening to tone changes. Behavioural accuracy improved already after day 1. [SMALL] — [doi:10.1111/ejn.70487](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejn.70487)

**Isolated tones are not the bottleneck; tone–word binding is**
- Pelzl, Lau, Guo & DeKeyser (SSLA, published online 2018). Advanced learners excelled at identifying tones in isolated syllables. But they were very inaccurate at rejecting disyllabic nonwords that differed only in tone, both in isolation and in sentences. The authors locate the difficulty in processing tones **lexically, especially in multisyllabic words**, not in perceiving them phonetically. — [doi:10.1017/s0272263117000444](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263117000444)
- Pelzl et al. (SSLA, 2020/2021). Even in a "best-case scenario" (clear words spoken in isolation), advanced learners made more lexical-decision errors when the tone was changed than when the vowel was changed. This held even for words whose tones they had identified correctly and confidently in an offline test. — [doi:10.1017/S027226312000039X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S027226312000039X)
- Pelzl, Carlson, Guo, Jackson & van Hell (2021, Bilingualism: Language and Cognition). Native listeners do **not** down-weight tone when a foreign-accented talker makes unsystematic tone errors. Primes with tone errors inhibited word recognition and raised accentedness ratings. So tone accuracy matters for intelligibility. — [doi:10.1017/S1366728920000280](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1366728920000280)
- Resource: "Tone Perfect", an MSU multimodal database of Mandarin syllables, has been proposed as a pedagogical tool for L2 tone study (possible source of multi-talker stimuli; licence not checked). — [DH2018 abstract](https://dh2018.adho.org/digital-humanities-under-your-fingertips-tone-perfect-as-a-pedagogical-tool-in-mandarin-chinese-second-language-studies-and-an-adaptable/)

### Inferences
- **HVPT module design**:
  - Task: four-choice tone identification. Response labels are tone diacritics, contour icons or numbers, not pictures and not colour alone.
  - Feedback: immediate correct/incorrect plus one tap to replay the native token.
  - Stimuli: real HSK-1 words, monosyllables first and disyllables soon after, recorded by several native talkers of both sexes.
  - Adaptivity: start with 1–2 talkers presented in blocks; switch to mixed multi-talker presentation once accuracy is high. This follows the aptitude × variability interactions above.
  - Dose: about 400 minutes in total (e.g. 20 × 20 min or 40 × 10 min) spread over weeks.
  - Probes: periodic retention and generalization checks with unheard talkers and words.
- Training must move from "which tone is this?" to **word-level tone tasks**, because the long-term problem is tone–word binding (Pelzl). Examples: "买 or 卖?" in a meaning context, lexical decision with tone-mismatched nonwords, tone dictation of whole words.
- Perception training does improve production, but only partly (d of about 0.5; roughly 10% on trained items). The app still needs a dedicated production module (Section 2).
- Learners who already have L2 experience gain less from HVPT. Front-load it in the HSK 1 phase.

### Gaps
- No HVPT study with **Italian L1** learners, and no randomized trial of a commercial mobile app, was found.
- Best session length and spacing for tone training are not established; the meta-analysis reports only total time.
- Godfroid et al. (2017): number of sessions and delayed-test interval not verified (paywalled full text).
- Zhang et al. (2021): it is unclear from the summary whether the generalization (0.72) and retention (1.09) effect sizes refer to high-vs-low-variability contrasts; not verified in full text.

## 2. Tone production: pitch-contour feedback, gesture, tone marks and colour coding, disyllables and tone sandhi, typical errors of Romance-L1 learners

### Takeaway
Visual F0 feedback, with learners comparing their own pitch curve to a native one, improves tone production in several studies. The strongest app-relevant evidence is Praat feedback that plays back the learner's *own voice* with corrected tones (Chen 2024). Observing and especially producing pitch gestures speeds up early tone and word learning, including in Catalan/Spanish speakers (Baills et al. 2019). Tone marks and pitch contours help; colour coding gives no added benefit. The real difficulty is in disyllables, tone pairs and sandhi, not isolated syllables. Beginners of Romance L1 (Italian among them) show lower tone accuracy than Germanic speakers, a bias towards Tone 1, very poor 3-3 sandhi, and systematic de-aspiration.

### Cited Findings
**Visual pitch-contour feedback**
- Chun, Jiang, Meyr & Yang (2015, Journal of Second Language Pronunciation). 35 learners created pitch curves of their own word tones and compared them with native curves, 20–25 min weekly for 9 weeks. Native raters judged tones better at post-test, and acoustic analyses showed improvement on some tones. — [Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jslp.1.1.04chu); earlier pilot: [Chun, Jiang & Avila, PSLLT 2012](https://iastatedigitalpress.com/psllt/article/id/15204)
- Chen, M. (2024, Computer Assisted Language Learning 37(3)). **44 beginners, 4-week word-level training.** One group got Praat-generated feedback: a visual tone-contour display plus playback of the corrected pronunciation **in their own voice**. The comparison group got similar feedback directly from a researcher. Both improved, but the Praat group made **significantly larger progress**. Learners preferred imitating corrected tones in their own voice and comparing contours directly. — [doi:10.1080/09588221.2022.2037652](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2022.2037652); [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1510695)
- Zhou & Olson (2023, Languages). 4 English-L1 beginners. Production of isolated words improved after visual feedback, with outcomes differing by tone. [SMALL, n = 4] — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/8/3/209)
- Pilot with beginners of mixed L1s: 6 hours of training on phrases and sentences with real-time pitch display (Sona Speech II). Native judges rated post-test tones significantly better. [SMALL / pilot] — [IGI Global](https://www.igi-global.com/article/auditory-visual-training-mandarin-tones/67123/)
- Perception-only training also improves production (Wang et al. 2003: +18%; pitch height lags behind contour), see Section 1 — [doi:10.1121/1.1531176](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1531176)

**Gesture / hand movement**
- Baills, Suárez-González, González-Fuente & Prieto (2019, SSLA 41(1):33–58). **106 participants, Catalan-dominant and all Spanish speakers (Romance L1)**, no tonal-language experience.
  - Exp. 1 (n = 49): passively watching pitch gestures vs no gestures.
  - Exp. 2 (n = 56): repeating words while watching gestures vs repeating while producing them.
  - The abstract reports a benefit of gesture observation over no gesture, and **a benefit of producing gestures over only observing them while repeating the words aloud**, for both tone identification and word learning.
  - The page summary also says a cross-experiment comparison found observation and production about equal (paraphrase, see Gaps).
  - — [doi:10.1017/S0272263118000074](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000074); [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/0b0e8937-b0e6-4d92-ac8d-b4fc92bb1335)
- Zhen, Van Hedger, Heald, Goldin-Meadow & Tian (2019, Cognition). English speakers learning Mandarin tones. Watching or performing gestures that match pitch direction in the vertical plane improved tone-category learning compared with audio only. When gestures were rotated into the horizontal plane, *performing* them beat *watching* them. — [doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2019.03.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2019.03.004)
- Morett & Chang (2015, Language, Cognition and Neuroscience). Pitch gestures improved English speakers' ability to tell apart the meanings of Mandarin words differing only in tone. **Semantic gestures (gestures showing word meaning) hindered it.** — [doi:10.1080/23273798.2014.923105](https://doi.org/10.1080/23273798.2014.923105)
- Cui et al. (2026, Frontiers in Neuroscience). 44 Vietnamese learners randomized to gesture training vs none, then tested on disyllabic tone-sandhi discrimination with fNIRS. The gesture group showed greater left-DLPFC responses. This is about mechanism; Vietnamese is a tonal L1. — [doi:10.3389/fnins.2026.1680211](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2026.1680211)

**Tone marks, contours, numbers, colour**
- Showalter & Hayes-Harb (2013, Second Language Research). English speakers with no Mandarin learned tone-contrasting nonwords with or without tone marks on the pinyin. **The tone-marks group learned the tone–word associations better**, and only they matched tone marks to auditory tones above chance. — [doi:10.1177/0267658313480154](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658313480154)
- Godfroid et al. (2017): **colour coding did slightly worse than numbers or contours**, and colour added to number or contour gave no extra gain (Section 1). — [doi:10.1111/lang.12246](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12246)
- Liu et al. (2011): pitch contours shown together with pinyin worked best (Section 1). — [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00673.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00673.x)
- In character learning, colour-marking radicals *interfered* with recognition (Hou & Jiang 2022, Section 4). Chung (2007) reports that "colour-coded prompts" (pinyin/English) improved learning and retention, but the abstract does not say what was coloured (Section 5).

**Disyllables, tone pairs and sandhi**
- Chang & Bowles (2015, JASA). In a word-learning experiment with inexperienced learners, **tones were learned less successfully in disyllables than in monosyllables**. The relative difficulty tracked how variable each tone is in context. In the short term variability can reduce learning. — [PDF](https://cbchang.com/wp-content/uploads/ChangBowles2015_JASA.pdf)
- iCALL corpus (Chen et al., Interspeech 2015). 305 beginners of European L1, about 1/3 Romance (French, Spanish, Italian), 90,841 read utterances from pinyin prompts [CORPUS] — [PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/chen15f_interspeech.pdf):
  - **overall 32.0% tone errors**;
  - production accuracy by tone: T1 72.6%, T2 67.7%, **T3 58.8%**, T4 68.4%, T5 (neutral) 75.5%;
  - **T3 is most often realized as T2 (19.3%)**;
  - 49% of 2-syllable words had at least one tone error, and 87% of sentences (only 13% of sentences of 5 or more syllables were tonally correct);
  - hardest tone pairs (% correct): **3-2: 39.9% (most often said as 2-2)**, 4-3: 46.4%, 2-2: 53.2%, 4-4: 55.2%;
  - **the 3-3 → 2-3 sandhi was realized correctly only 9.6% of the time.**
- Conflict: a search summary of the earlier iCALL analysis gave a T3 *error* rate of 51.90%, versus the 41.2% error (58.8% accuracy) in the 2015 paper. The 2015 paper uses re-checked second-round transcriptions, so prefer it. — [ISCA 2015](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/chen15f_interspeech.html)
- Chen, He, Wayland, Yang, Li & Yuen (2019, Speech Communication 115). Intermediate American-English and Cantonese learners did a wug test (form disyllables from real and nonsense morphemes). They applied both third-tone sandhi and half-third sandhi on real and novel words alike, i.e. the rules are productive, but with **less detailed and less accurate tonal contours than natives**. — [PDF](https://slam.lin.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/106/2020/01/Chen_2019_Mechanismsoftonesandhiruleapplicationbytonalandnon-tonalnon-nativespeakers.pdf)
- A thesis on T3 sandhi in L2, L3 and heritage learners measured comprehensibility and accentedness. [SMALL / thesis] — [UVic](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/bbb73568-e539-455c-a800-d979982671aa)

**Romance-L1 (Italian, Spanish, French) error profile**
- iCALL [CORPUS] — [PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/chen15f_interspeech.pdf):
  - tone accuracy was higher for Germanic speakers than for Romance or Slavic speakers (p < 0.05);
  - **Romance speakers, Slavic speakers and women showed a bias to produce Tone 1**; French speakers most of all (the authors relate this to French having no lexical stress, unlike Italian);
  - fluency ratings: Germanic > Slavic > Romance;
  - segments: overall phone error rate 5.0%, with **6 of the top 10 errors involving aspiration**;
  - top phone errors by rate: c (16.4%), q (10.5%), ch (10.2%), z (9.8%), zh (8.2%), s (7.7%), t (7.4%), p (6.6%), -iu (6.1%), j (6.0%); t and p were mostly replaced by their unaspirated counterparts d and b;
  - Romance speakers de-aspirate (Romance languages have no aspiration contrast);
  - for Italian speakers the paper notes one exception for the ch/zh pair; the direction is ambiguous in the extracted text.
- Hao, Gong & Zhang (2023, Interspeech). 25 native Spanish speakers, Mandarin continuous speech. **T3 was hardest to perceive; difficulty order T4 < T1 < T2 < T3.** Tones in stressed syllables were perceived better than in unstressed ones. Tone × context interactions caused confusion, **especially when T2 and T3 were adjacent**. — [ISCA](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/hao23_interspeech.html)
- Rallo Fabra, Liu, Chen & Wayland (2021, book chapter). 12 Spanish-L1 school children (mean age 9.5) learning in class. F0 *shapes* differed from natives for all four tones; F0 *height* differed only for T2 and T3. The authors suggest pitch contour is harder than pitch height. [SMALL, children] — [UIB](https://portalinvestigacio.uib.es/documentos/68de3350487e182b7b98bccd)
- University of Pisa thesis (2016), "Tone Acquisition of Mandarin Chinese by Italian Adult Learners", beginners to advanced. Perception improved with level for monosyllables and isolated disyllables, **but for disyllables in context advanced learners did worse than intermediates**. Production of monosyllables improved with level; disyllables in words and sentences gave "extremely complex" results. [SMALL / thesis] — [UniPi ETD](https://etd.adm.unipi.it/t/etd-09152016-212152)
- Sparvoli (2014, Proceedings of the Italian Association for Chinese Studies, AISC). Proposes **articulatory instructions for Italian learners**, especially for T2 and T4, built on Italian phonology. Uses the Italian /ɛ/–/e/ and /ɔ/–/o/ contrasts as scaffolding and groups tones into "constant" (T1, T3) and "profile" (T2, T4) tones. [proposal, not tested] — [CRIS Unibo](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/782401)
- Tang & Gili Fivela (2015, Studi AISV). Italian learners perceiving Mandarin vowels such as /y/ and /ɤ/ tend to map them onto Italian categories. Identification correlates with L2 exposure, but weakly. Knowing French or German (which have /y/) helped only partly. [SMALL] — [PDF](https://www.aisv.it/StudiAISV/2015/vol_1/014_TangGiliFivela.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Production module**:
  - record the learner, then show their F0 contour over a native contour;
  - optionally play back a resynthesized version of the learner's own voice with the target contour (closest to Chen 2024);
  - work at word level, with HSK-1 disyllables as the core unit.
- **Gesture**: show hand or arrow animations of the contour and ask the learner to trace the contour with a finger on screen while saying the word. Finger-tracing is *untested*; it is the nearest mobile equivalent to producing gestures. Use only gestures that encode pitch, never gestures that encode meaning (Morett & Chang).
- **Visual tone code**: keep tone diacritics on pinyin, plus optional contour icons or numbers, at least through HSK 1–2. Make tone colour **optional or off by default**, or A/B-test it; there is no evidence it adds anything to tone perception.
- **Drill priorities for Italian learners** (inferred from the Romance-L1 data; not validated on Italians):
  - (a) T3 as a *low* tone (half-third) and the T2/T3 contrast, including the 3-2 and 3-3 pairs;
  - (b) explicit, drilled 3-3 sandhi and the HSK-1 sandhi of 不 and 一;
  - (c) counter the bias towards T1;
  - (d) aspiration contrasts b/p, d/t, g/k, z/c, zh/ch, j/q, which are new for Italians;
  - (e) all 20 disyllabic tone combinations, including the neutral tone.
- Because advanced Italian learners did worse on disyllables in context (Pisa thesis) and context effects are strong (Chang & Bowles; Hao et al.), drills should go up to phrases and sentences, not stop at isolated syllables.

### Gaps
- No published, peer-reviewed controlled training study with **Italian adults** was found, only a thesis, a proposal paper and the Romance subset of iCALL. Italian-specific results from iCALL are not broken out beyond the ch/zh note.
- No meta-analysis of gesture-based tone training was found; the evidence is several separate experiments.
- Baills et al. (2019): the "observation and production equivalent" claim comes from a page summary and conflicts with the abstract's "production > observation" statement for Exp. 2. Check the full text.
- No study comparing colour coding of *characters by tone* (the common app feature) for tone *memory* was found.
- No controlled study comparing tone-pair-based vs syllable-based curricula was found. Chinese-language work on 双音节声调教学 could not be reached (CNKI blocked).

## 3. Automatic tone recognition and pronunciation assessment for L2 Mandarin

### Takeaway
Research systems detect tone mispronunciations in *read* L2 Mandarin with equal error rates of about 4–6% (BLSTM, 2018). Recent wav2vec2-based diagnosis models, with phonological features covering both segments and tones, cut false acceptances further (Interspeech 2026). L2 tones often fall *between* categories, even for human annotators, so the reliable design is probabilistic feedback with a visual contour, not a binary right/wrong verdict. No independent accuracy figures for commercial engines were found.

### Cited Findings
- iCALL corpus (A*STAR I²R): 305 European-L1 speakers, 142 h, 90,841 utterances with phonetic and tonal transcription and fluency ratings. Second-round annotators had to force a choice for ambiguous tones but flag them as ambiguous (e.g. a rise-fall contour that matches no Mandarin tone). An oracle decision-tree model using the human transcriptions predicted fluency ratings at r = 0.87 against human scores. [CORPUS] — [ISCA PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/chen15f_interspeech.pdf)
- Chen, Siniscalchi, Lee & Li, "Improving Mandarin tone mispronunciation detection for non-native learners with soft-target tone labels and BLSTM-based deep models" (ICASSP 2018). Moving from DNN to BLSTM tone models and replacing one-hot targets with probabilistic "soft" targets, because learner tones often sit between canonical categories, lowered equal error rate from 5.77% to 4.86%, and to **4.34% with system combination (24.78% relative reduction)**. The corpus is not named in the summary. — [A*STAR OAR](https://oar.a-star.edu.sg/communities-collections/articles/14019)
- A related BLSTM-GOP soft-target report (search summary only): EER 7.58% → 5.83% → 5.16% with BLSTM verifiers; AUC up to 98.47%. Attribution unclear, treat as unverified. — [UniPA repository record](https://iris.unipa.it/handle/10447/641418)
- Chen, Shahin & Ahmed (Interspeech 2026, arXiv June 2026). Wav2Vec2-CTC model for diagnosing mispronunciations that represents both segmental and tonal features. Against a phoneme-only baseline it lowered **False Acceptance Rate by 10.1%** and **Diagnostic Error Rate by 23.6%**, and gives more interpretable diagnostic feedback. Absolute tone accuracy is not given in the abstract. — [arXiv 2606.22022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22022)
- Chen (2024): automatic Praat-based contour plus own-voice feedback beat human feedback in a 4-week training study (Section 2). — [doi:10.1080/09588221.2022.2037652](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2022.2037652)

### Inferences
- Lab EERs of about 4–6% come from **read speech with known target text** (text-dependent) recorded in quiet rooms. Expect worse results with phone microphones, noise and spontaneous speech. Design feedback that is forgiving (confidence bands, "almost T3"), and test it on in-house recordings of Italian learners.
- The safest approach is text-prompted assessment of known HSK words: F0 extraction plus a tone classifier, with the contour shown visually. That combines reasonable automatic accuracy with contour feedback, which has supporting evidence in its own right.
- Because L2 tones are often ambiguous between categories (iCALL, soft-target work), report a probability per tone rather than a forced category. This keeps errors that native listeners penalize (Pelzl 2021) from being passed as correct.

### Gaps
- No independent peer-reviewed evaluation of commercial Mandarin scoring engines (iFlytek, SpeechSuper, Chivox and similar) for L2 learners, or of their agreement with human raters, was found.
- No published tone-recognition accuracy specifically for Italian-L1 speech was found.
- arXiv was rate-limited, so recent work on wav2vec2/Whisper-based L2 tone classification (2023–2026) could not be surveyed systematically.

## 4. Characters: radicals and components, orthographic awareness, mnemonics, stroke order, handwriting vs typing vs recognition-only, character–sound–meaning binding

### Takeaway
Pointing out components *at first exposure* and teaching high-frequency semantic radicals helps memory and transfers to new characters. Learners also build orthographic sensitivity implicitly and quickly. But showing every sub-character cue at once (coloured radicals plus stroke animations) can overload learners during recognition. Handwriting best supports orthographic form and the ability to reproduce characters. Reading supports recall of meaning and sound, and pinyin typing supports phonology. So HSK 1 (recognition only) and HSK 2+ (handwriting) call for different practice. Learner-generated or key-image mnemonics help, but imposed imagery can be forgotten faster. Form is acquired before stable links to sound and meaning.

### Cited Findings
**Radicals, components and orthographic awareness**
- Taft & Chung (1999, Psychologia 42(4):243–251). Australian learners with no Chinese learned 24 two-radical characters with meanings. Four groups: radicals taught before (15 min), radicals pointed out at first presentation ("Radicals Early"), at the third presentation ("Late"), or not at all. **Memory was best for Radicals Early.** The authors conclude radicals should be highlighted when a character is first presented. [EXP] — [Psychologia abstract](https://cogpsy.educ.kyoto-u.ac.jp/psychologia/ab/4245.htm)
- Kuo et al. (2015, Frontiers in Psychology). Adolescent English-speaking beginners in the US. Characters with radicals and lower visual complexity were easier to learn. Radical awareness was associated with learning all characters, more strongly those containing radicals. — [PMC4550700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4550700)
- Nguyen, Zhang, Li, Wu & Cheng (2017, Frontiers in Psychology). 54 Vietnamese undergraduates with 1 year of Chinese. "Old-for-new" semantic-radical teaching improved sentence-cloze inference of unfamiliar characters, and **transferred to characters with radicals that had not been taught**; the control group did not improve. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01846](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01846)
- Zhou (2023, Journal of Psycholinguistic Research). Studies the direct and indirect contributions of radical knowledge and character recognition to L2 Chinese passage-level reading comprehension. The retrieved abstract was cut off before the results. — [doi:10.1007/s10936-022-09880-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-022-09880-w)
- Shen (2005, System 33(1)). Survey of 95 university non-native learners from beginner to advanced. Found 30 commonly used strategies (25 cognitive, 5 metacognitive). The top two dealt with tone production and the weak link between characters and pronunciation. For beginners, learning each radical "would not be perceived as useful" because their orthographic knowledge is still limited. — [doi:10.1016/j.system.2004.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2004.11.001); details from [CSL journal summary](https://benjamins.com/catalog/csl.51.2.01zah)
- Wang, Perfetti & Liu (2003, Scientific Studies of Reading 7(2)). Adult English-speaking learners quickly acquired knowledge of character structure. With limited vocabulary and **no explicit instruction** they were already sensitive to curriculum frequency and internal composition (as described in follow-up work). — [Perfetti lab PDF citing it](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/Implicit%20and%20explicit%20learning%20of%20ortho-%20Wang,%20Liu.pdf)
- Hou & Jiang (2022, Frontiers in Psychology). 40 L2 learners with alphabetic L1s, high and low level, 2 × 2 design. **Radical markings in a different colour slowed recognition and lowered accuracy.** Stroke-order animations also lowered recognition accuracy, with no effect on character–meaning matching. The authors invoke cognitive overload and split attention, and suggest matching the presentation to the learning goal (simple presentation when the goal is recognition). [EXP, n = 40] — [PMC9403612](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9403612/)
- Yan, Fan, Di, Havlin & Wu (2013, PLoS ONE). Modelled characters as a frequency-weighted network of structural (component) relations. A learning order that weighs both frequency and position in the component hierarchy (the "DNW" strategy) **outperformed the orders used in mainstream L1 and L2 textbooks** in simulation. Computational, not tested on learners. — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0069745](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0069745)

**Mnemonics and stories**
- Wang & Thomas (1992, Language Learning 42(3):359–376). Two studies: imagery mnemonics vs rote learning of character–English pairs, delays of 2 days and 1 week. Mnemonics gave no lasting advantage, and **forgetting was greater under mnemonic learning**. The authors attribute this to experimenter-imposed rather than self-generated encodings. — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus1990/1047)
- Chang, Chen, Perfetti & Chen (2019, Journal of Teaching Chinese as a Foreign Language 16(2)). 27 US students with no Chinese learned 48 characters over 3 sessions with key-image mnemonics, etymology, or stroke sequence. The form + meaning methods (key-image, etymology) were better for recognizing and reproducing forms. **Key-image was best for complex characters and for reproduction, and its benefit persisted at delayed tests.** [SMALL] — [NTNU Scholars](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effect-of-key-image-mnemonics-to-support-character-learning-o/)

**Writing vs typing vs reading**
- Guan, Liu, Chan, Ye & Perfetti (2011, Journal of Educational Psychology).
  - Exp. 1: adult learners with an online writing tutor (handwriting) vs a reading-only tutor. **Writing improved word recognition and character–meaning links, but not character–sound links.**
  - Exp. 2 added a pinyin typing tutor. It replicated the writing effects; typing supported only phonological representations and the character–sound link.
  - The authors conclude that writing strengthens orthography and alphabetic coding strengthens phonology.
  - — [Perfetti lab PDF](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/Writing%20strengthens%20Guan%20JEP.pdf)
- Xu, Chang, Zhang & Perfetti (2013, Foreign Language Annals). Learners with some orthographic knowledge. **Writing and animation gave better form recognition. Reading gave better recall of meaning and sound. Animation beat writing on meaning recall. Writing was best for reproducing characters from memory.** — [NTNU Scholars](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/reading-writing-and-animation-in-character-learning-in-chinese-as-2/)
- Lyu, Lai, Lin & Gong (2021, International Journal of Educational Research 106:101740), "Comparison studies of typing and handwriting in Chinese language learning: A synthetic review". Title and authors verified. A search summary attributes to this line of work the finding that typing has a greater effect on phonological recognition and phonology–orthography mapping than handwriting. Abstract not verified directly. — [doi:10.1016/j.ijer.2021.101740](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijer.2021.101740)
- Chai & Ma (2021, Frontiers in Psychology), **HSK big data: 74,362 learners with 67 L1s**. Character-writing proficiency significantly predicted reading proficiency, and interacted with the learner's language distance from Chinese. Correlational. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2021.779190](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.779190)
- Chiu (2024, University of Delaware dissertation). 23 boarding-school learners, 5 weeks. Handwriting-then-typing beat typing-then-handwriting on vocabulary, character knowledge and sentence composition. [SMALL] — [UDSpace](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/2dbf93f4-f0d2-4c62-98f2-3af36a707171)
- Zhang & Xing (2023, Frontiers in Psychology). 33,856 written characters from 2,116 learners with 7 L1s. Successful production rested on orthography–phonology–semantics connections, **especially the orthography–semantics link**. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1076810](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1076810)

**Stroke order**
- Sun, Wang & Yu (2024, ChinaXiv preprint). Computer-assisted stroke-order learning, comparing correct, random and no stroke order. Correct order gave **more accurate writing, especially for characters with few strokes, and a trend towards faster writing**. "The impact on writing correctly is greater than on writing quickly." [SMALL / preprint, n not given] — [ChinaXiv](https://psych.chinaxiv.org/abs/202410.00117v2)
- Press report of the same work: participants practising correct stroke order recognized and wrote characters "15 percent faster", with longer retention. [SECONDARY, unverified] — [China Daily](https://epaper.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202503/04/WS67c6389da310f4a5fb061eef.html)

**Character–sound–meaning binding**
- Liu, Wang & Perfetti (2007, Memory & Cognition). Primed naming with US learners at the end of the 1st and 2nd terms. After term 1 only orthographic primes helped. After term 2 orthographic facilitation had gone and **semantic** primes helped. This supports a threshold-style model: form becomes stable first, then form–meaning links; form–sound links are weak. — [doi:10.3758/bf03193287](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03193287)

### Inferences
- **Character card design**:
  - on first exposure, decompose the character into components with brief meaning or sound hints (Taft & Chung);
  - teach high-frequency semantic radicals as reusable "keys" (Nguyen);
  - in later recognition reviews, show the bare character; component colours and stroke animations are available **on demand**, not as permanent overlays (Hou & Jiang).
- **Mnemonics**: give a key-image or story as a scaffold, invite learners to write their own, and always follow up with spaced retrieval practice. This guards against the faster forgetting seen with imposed mnemonics.
- **HSK 1 (recognition only)**: recognition drills, pinyin typing (good for character–sound links) and reading in context are enough. **From HSK 2, where handwriting is required**: add recall-writing practice (write from meaning or sound with stroke-order guidance, then fade the guide), because only writing reliably builds reproduction.
- **Order of new characters**: sort by frequency × component reuse (network approach) and group by shared components, within the constraints of the HSK list.
- **Characters per day**: no empirical guideline was found. As plain arithmetic, the 246 HSK-1 characters over 8–12 weeks is about 3–4 new characters a day; set intake by spaced-repetition review load, not by research evidence.

### Gaps
- No study found that establishes an optimal number of new characters per day or session for adult L2 learners.
- No usable data on **component frequency** (how many components cover HSK characters) was retrieved. GB13000.1 component standards and similar Chinese sources were not reachable.
- Lyu et al. (2021): conclusions and number of reviewed studies not verified (Elsevier and HKU pages blocked).
- Stroke-order evidence is thin: one preprint plus a press report. Animation results conflict (Xu et al. 2013 positive for form recognition; Hou & Jiang 2022 negative for recognition accuracy).
- Kuo & Hooper (2004) and Shen (2004, 2010) on mnemonic and elaborative coding were not retrieved.

## 5. Pinyin, word segmentation and reading development (pinyin help or hindrance; pinyin fading; eye-tracking; graded readers and extensive reading; coverage)

### Takeaway
Pinyin with tone marks helps learners link sounds and tones to new words. But showing it *at the same time* as the character competes for attention. Novices learn characters better when the character comes first and pinyin/meaning appear after a short delay, when pinyin is stacked vertically, or when redundant on-screen pinyin is removed. Pinyin's Latin letters also cause pronunciation errors because learners read them with L1 values, which matters for Italians. Spacing between *words* helps L2 readers. Extensive-reading evidence for Chinese is mostly about attitudes; no Chinese-specific lexical coverage threshold was found.

### Cited Findings
**Pinyin: help or hindrance**
- Chung (2007, Educational Psychology). High-school learners. Learning of meaning and pronunciation **went faster when the character was shown before the pinyin/English prompts rather than after them**. Adding colour-coded prompts improved learning. **Two-week retention was best for character first plus colour coding.** Interpreted as avoiding split attention. — [doi:10.1080/01443410601061306](https://doi.org/10.1080/01443410601061306)
- A search summary of Chung's work recommends showing the character 5 seconds before its pinyin and English, rather than all at once, for early-stage learners (likely the 2002/2003 studies). — [HKU Hub record](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/42601)
- Lee & Kalyuga (2011, Language Learning 61(4)). Compared vertical vs horizontal layouts of character, pinyin and English. **Vertical layout was significantly better**, attributed to removing split attention. The commonly used horizontal layout "may impose high levels of cognitive load and hinder the learning of characters at the beginner level". — [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00666.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00666.x)
- Lee & Kalyuga (2011, ICCE proceedings). Character + pronunciation vs character + pinyin + pronunciation. **For learners with less prior knowledge, character + audio *without* written pinyin was better (redundancy effect).** No difference for more advanced learners (expertise reversal). — [ICCE](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/view/2607)
- Showalter & Hayes-Harb (2013): tone marks on pinyin help learners associate tones with new words (Section 2). — [doi:10.1177/0267658313480154](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658313480154)
- Hayes-Harb & Cheng (2016, Frontiers in Psychology). English speakers learned Mandarin words through pinyin or zhuyin. **The zhuyin group did better, because the pinyin group struggled with "incongruent" items**, where a familiar letter stands for a new sound (e.g. ‹x› = [ɕ]). The groups did not differ in hearing the sounds, so the effect comes from the written input. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00785](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00785)
- Bassetti (2006, Written Language & Literacy 9(1)). Beginners do not count or segment the main vowel in syllables whose pinyin spelling omits it (e.g. ‹iu›, ‹ui›, ‹un›). Pinyin is reinterpreted through L1 letter–sound rules, which explains why learners leave out these vowels when speaking. — [prepublication PDF](https://iris.unimo.it/retrieve/7a1ac781-e849-493e-a314-f4f9bab2936f/Bassetti2006WLLprepub.pdf); [Benjamins](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/wll.9.1.07bas)
- iCALL: beginners reading pinyin prompts mispronounced c, q, ch, z, zh, s, t, p, -iu and j most often (Section 2). — [PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/chen15f_interspeech.pdf)

**Eye-tracking on pinyin**
- Wang (2022, BYU thesis), sentences with pinyin above characters. **University beginners spent more time and fixations on unfamiliar and new characters than on pinyin**, i.e. they used pinyin strategically, and most found pinyin distracting. Elementary immersion children looked more at pinyin than at characters and treated it as a crutch. [SMALL / thesis] — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/9461/)
- "Eye movements of online Chinese learners" (Open University). When pinyin and characters were both shown, all beginner and lower-intermediate participants looked at the pinyin to some degree. [search-snippet level; page 403] — [ORO](https://oro.open.ac.uk/40827)

**Delaying characters**
- Ye (2013, Foreign Language Annals). Survey of 914 students and 192 instructors in US university programs. **Most programs did not delay characters**, and most respondents thought characters should start near the beginning of the first semester. Views shifted once reasons for and against delay were presented. — [doi:10.1111/flan.12049](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12049)
- Packard (1990, Modern Language Journal), "Effects of time lag in the introduction of characters": the classic experiment on delayed character introduction. Abstract not retrieved. — [doi:10.1111/j.1540-4781.1990.tb02562.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1990.tb02562.x)
- He (2023). Delaying only *writing*, until after listening, speaking and reading, worked better for beginners than delaying all literacy. Low-profile journal. [SMALL / low tier] — [doi:10.54097/jeer.v2i2.6663](https://doi.org/10.54097/jeer.v2i2.6663)

**Word segmentation**
- Shen, Liversedge et al. (2012, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied). American, Korean, Japanese and Thai learners. **Word-spaced text disrupted reading least.** Unspaced text was read more slowly than character-spaced text, and spacing that cut across words disrupted reading most. The pattern was the same across L1s. Marking word boundaries speeds word identification, so words are psychologically real for L2 readers. — [doi:10.1037/a0027485](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027485)
- Bassetti (2009, Applied Psycholinguistics). Spacing helped English CSL readers read **pinyin**, but did not affect **character** reading in English or Chinese readers. — [doi:10.1017/s0142716409990105](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0142716409990105)
- Bassetti & Lu (2016, IRAL). 12 English CSL readers read faster with word spacing in a gap-filling task. [SMALL] — [doi:10.1515/iral-2016-0014](https://doi.org/10.1515/iral-2016-0014)
- Cui (2023, Frontiers in Psychology). 24 intermediate CSL learners vs 20 natives, eye-tracking. Learners read word-spaced texts faster, with more gazes and regressions on unspaced text. **Spacing across words and spacing pinyin-style interfered with reading.** Reviews prior evidence that spacing helps comprehension, speed and vocabulary learning. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.783960](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.783960)

**Graded readers, extensive reading (ER) and coverage**
- Zhou & Day (2023, Reading in a Foreign Language 35(2)). 13-week ER program at a US liberal-arts college. ER significantly improved L2 Chinese readers' **confidence and commitment**. Students *reported* gains in fluency, comprehension, vocabulary and character recognition. No objective outcome measures in the abstract. — [NFLRC](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/574)
- BUAP licentiate thesis (2023), Mexican beginners (likely Spanish L1). Perceived ER benefits for word recognition and vocabulary. [SMALL / thesis] — [BUAP](https://repositorioinstitucional.buap.mx/items/e44042d1-54db-4ea0-ace2-1a69bb014eae)
- Mandarin Companion (publisher) justifies graded readers with general ER theory; no Chinese-specific trial is cited there. [SECONDARY / commercial] — [Mandarin Companion](https://mandarincompanion.com/?p=427)
- Lexical coverage thresholds from *English* L2 research: Hu & Nation (2000) estimated 95–98% known words for adequate comprehension; other figures range from 90% to 98% depending on the comprehension required. — [Laufer, "Lexical threshold revisited"](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/eaead47e-853e-4797-ba6f-596df150c1d7)
- Character text coverage (modern Chinese) [SECONDARY]: 100 characters cover about 42%, 500 about 77.5%, 1,000 about 90.5%, 2,000 about 97.8%, 3,000 about 99.5% (Xing 2007). State Language Commission figures: about 590 characters for 80% coverage, 940 for 90%, 2,400 for 99%. — [Wikipedia: Chinese character frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character_frequency)

### Inferences
- **Flashcards**: show the character first, then pinyin with tone marks and the meaning after about 1–5 s or on tap. Stack pinyin above the character, never side by side (Lee & Kalyuga). For novices, audio can replace written pinyin once pinyin decoding is solid.
- **Pinyin fading in readers**: turn on per-word pinyin for unknown or new words only, tap-to-reveal for words that are known or being learned, and none for mastered words. This adapts to expertise, a practical form of expertise reversal. A full pinyin-free mode should be required in HSK test simulations.
- **Pinyin teaching for Italians** needs an explicit "false friends" unit pairing each item with audio contrasts, because pinyin spelling causes systematic L1-based errors (Bassetti; Hayes-Harb & Cheng; iCALL). Items:
  - b/d/g are voiceless unaspirated, not Italian voiced /b d g/;
  - p/t/k are aspirated;
  - z = [ts], c = [tsʰ], q = [tɕʰ], x = [ɕ], j = [tɕ], zh/ch/sh/r;
  - e = [ɤ], ü (also written u after j/q/x/y);
  - the hidden vowels in -iu, -ui, -un;
  - -ian = [iɛn].
- **Reader UI**: word-segmented display (tap highlights the whole word, or optional thin word spacing), with an option to switch to normal unspaced text. Never use spacing that cuts across words.
- **Graded readers for HSK 1–2**: target about 95–98% known *words*, a threshold borrowed from English; add a "% known" indicator. The 246 HSK-1 characters, if they were among the most frequent, would cover something between about 42% (100 characters) and about 77% (500 characters) of running text. Real reading therefore needs heavily controlled texts at this level.

### Gaps
- Chung (2002) and Chung (2003): abstracts not retrieved; the "5 s delay" detail is from a search summary.
- No controlled study of **gradual pinyin fading** in L2 Chinese reading was found.
- No Chinese-specific **lexical coverage** threshold study was found; Chinese-language work (词汇覆盖率) could not be searched.
- No controlled study of the effect of Chinese graded readers on objective outcomes (reading rate, vocabulary) was found; existing work is attitudinal or small.
- Everson (1998) on naming and knowing, and the on-screen pinyin "expertise reversal" paper in Computers in Human Behavior, were not retrieved.

## 6. Chinese vocabulary: compounds, morpheme (morphological) awareness, confusable words, measure words

### Takeaway
Morphological awareness, i.e. knowing how compounds are built from morphemes and affixoids, consistently predicts adult L2 Chinese word inference and reading. The effect is mostly *indirect*, through vocabulary knowledge and lexical inference. Classroom intervention studies are rare. Measure words are a known stumbling block: learners overuse 个. No usable research on confusable words was reached.

### Cited Findings
- Ke & Koda (2017, Modern Language Journal 101(4):742–755). 50 English-speaking adults. Learners were sensitive to the morphological structure of unknown multi-character words and **inferred meanings better when words contained affixoids and familiar bases**. L1 morphological awareness transferred to L2. L2 morphological awareness helped inference indirectly, through L2 linguistic knowledge. — [doi:10.1111/modl.12428](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12428)
- Zhang, Zhang, Li & Zhang (2021, Frontiers in Psychology). 447 adult L2 learners. Morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge together predicted reading comprehension **through lexical inference**. Morphological awareness in written form activates both morphological and orthographic information. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2021.736933](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.736933)
- Zhou (2022, Journal of Psycholinguistic Research). 209 learners. Morphological awareness (morpheme discrimination, compound structure) fed vocabulary knowledge, which predicted reading; the effect on reading was indirect. — [doi:10.1007/s10936-021-09810-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-021-09810-2)
- Zhang (2019, Journal of Psycholinguistic Research). 61 English-L1 vs 68 Thai-L1 learners. Thai readers did better on compound awareness after controlling for vocabulary, so L1 script type shapes L2 morphological awareness. — [doi:10.1007/s10936-018-9593-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-018-9593-4)
- Ke & Koda (2019, Applied Linguistics), "Is vocabulary knowledge sufficient for word-meaning inference?". Argues for a role of morphological awareness beyond vocabulary. Abstract not retrieved. — [PolyU record](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/is-vocabulary-knowledge-sufficient-for-word-meaning-inference-an-/)
- Glimois (2023, Revista de Estudios Asiáticos, review of classifier acquisition). Learners **default to the general classifier 个**. Shape and mensural classifiers are particularly hard for adults, because the system requires sorting nouns into semantic categories. The review recommends explicit instruction plus attention to classifiers during comprehension (input processing). — [UCR](https://archivo.revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/riea/article/view/52212)
- Liu (2018, Michigan State University dissertation) on English speakers' comprehension and production of classifiers (elicited production, self-paced reading, lexical decision). — [MSU](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/19602)

### Inferences
- Build **morpheme families** into the HSK word graph (e.g. 电 → 电话/电视/电脑/电影; 学 → 学生/学校/学习) with "guess the meaning" items for unseen compounds. This trains lexical inference, the route by which morphological awareness pays off for reading.
- **Measure words**: drill classifier + noun pairs as chunks (一本书, 一杯茶), group nouns by classifier category (本: bound items such as books; 张: flat objects), and include contrast items that block the 个 default.
- Since HSK 1 has few morphemes, morphology work becomes worthwhile mostly from HSK 2–3 onward. At HSK 1, focus on showing morpheme reuse.

### Gaps
- Few **intervention** studies teaching morphology to adult CFL learners were found; most evidence is correlational. Radical teaching (Nguyen 2017) is the closest intervention.
- No research on **confusable words** (易混淆词, e.g. Zhang Bo's work) could be retrieved; the Chinese databases were blocked.
- No study comparing ways to teach classifiers in apps was found.

## 7. Research on HSK preparation and HSK learners (including Chinese-language literature)

### Takeaway
Direct research on *HSK preparation methods* is scarce. The main data-rich study is a correlational HSK big-data analysis linking character-writing proficiency to reading. Details of the new HSK (HSK 3.0) should be checked against the official syllabus: secondary sources give 300 words for HSK 1, but character counts differ from the 246 in the brief. Chinese-language academic databases could not be reached.

### Cited Findings
- Chai & Ma (2021, Frontiers in Psychology). **74,362 HSK test-takers with 67 L1s.** Character-writing proficiency significantly predicted reading proficiency, and interacted with the learner's language distance from Chinese; cultural background did not interact. Correlational. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2021.779190](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.779190)
- Zhang & Xing (2023): large learner-writing dataset (2,116 learners, 33,856 characters) on character production errors (Section 4). The data source (e.g. an HSK composition corpus) is not stated in the abstract. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1076810](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1076810)
- Zhou & Goh (2025, PLoS ONE). 32 international students. A "seamless" vocabulary-learning framework, with lexical frequency profiles measured at HSK level 4. [SMALL] — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0319285](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0319285)
- New HSK (HSK 3.0), secondary sources [SECONDARY]:
  - official exam outline said to be released in **November 2025**, formal start **14 July 2026**; regular HSK 1–6 sittings in 2026 still partly on the HSK 2.0 format during the transition;
  - **HSK 1 = 300 words** (double the 150 of HSK 2.0), HSK 2 = +200 (500 cumulative), HSK 3 = +500 (1,000 cumulative);
  - HSK 1 requires no handwriting, only character recognition and pinyin input;

### Inferences
- The HSK big-data link between handwriting and reading supports adding handwriting practice from HSK 2. The direction of cause is unknown.
- Lacking HSK-specific methods research, the app should combine the general evidence above with the official word and character lists and the exam format. That means listening with tones; reading characters without pinyin from the levels where the test shows no pinyin; and handwriting from HSK 2 if the official syllabus confirms it.

### Gaps
- **CNKI, Baidu Scholar and similar were blocked (403)**, and the web-search budget ran out before Chinese-language queries (声调教学, 汉字教学, 汉语水平考试 备考, 二语习得) could be done. Chinese-language findings, e.g. from 语言教学与研究, 世界汉语教学, 汉语学习 and 华语文教学研究, are therefore largely missing. An exception is Chang et al. (2019) in the Taiwan-based 華語文教學研究 (Section 4).
- The official HSK 3.0 figures (HSK-1 character count, 246 vs about 200; whether handwriting starts at HSK 2) are not confirmed from the official syllabus (chinesetest.cn / CTI). The brief's figures should be checked by whoever covers the exam specifications.
- No controlled study of HSK test-preparation methods (e.g. washback studies, effectiveness of HSK apps) was found.
